Moriarty on Cardano

Bring the required proofs to a Cardano transaction.

Moriarty is a language for financial contracts. Webs let a Cardano application attach proofs to a transaction, so its validator can check that the contract’s rules were followed.

For an invoice payment, that means checking how much reaches the seller and how much is still owed after the fee. The example below follows those amounts through a single payment.

Cash and promises are separate accounts

A duty records an amount still owed by one party to another. Here, escrow owes the seller 100 CASH for the invoice and owes the buyer a separate 10 CASH reserve. A payment changes custody and may discharge a duty; the two changes need to be recorded separately.

For this payment, the seller receives 35 CASH and the fee recipient receives 2. Escrow loses 37 CASH in total, leaving 75. Only the seller’s payment reduces the invoice: 100 − 35 = 65. The reserve stays at 10, so the agreement continues with 75 CASH owed.

What happens after another payment?

Each partial payment charges another 2 CASH. The extra 2 in the 112-CASH illustration covers one partial only. A later payment needs its own funding rule. The source’s finish() pays and discharges the remaining invoice; it leaves the reserve obligation in place. That source has no reserve-return action, so paying the invoice does not complete the entire agreement.

What are Webs and Strands?

The payment above has several conditions to check. These are the names for the evidence that travels with it.

Strand

A Strand carries one Groth16 proof, the claim it proves and the information that ties it to a particular transaction. For an invoice, its claim might cover the payment calculation and the unpaid balance.

Web

A Web collects the Strands a contract requires for a transaction. The contract sets those requirements. Settlement needs every required condition covered by a matching Strand.

One payment can have several checks and one proof

For the invoice walkthrough, one whole-execution Strand covers the partial-payment action. Its payment, fee and remaining-duty checks are parts of that action. The Web contains that Strand; it is not three proofs made by renaming three checks.

The issuance route then creates one claim output in one Issue transaction, followed by one settlement transaction. These are the counts for this teaching design. A different decomposition requires real separate relations and an explicitly compatible consumer.

How can an application reuse a kind of claim?

A claim specification describes a kind of claim: what it asserts, which inputs it uses and which compiled rules it must satisfy. It can be reused across transactions; a proof for an old state cannot simply be reused for a changed one.

A proof of the calculation still needs a transaction

A condition is something the contract requires, such as a correct fee calculation. A Strand contains a Groth16 proof, its public claim and the information tying it to this transaction. One proof can cover several conditions. The settlement validator rebuilds the claim from the transaction and checks that it matches the claim whose proof was verified. An external price or delivery record still needs an authenticated source and a freshness rule.

03On Cardano
From a proof to a settled payment

Proof production and ledger settlement are different events. The claim-output route connects them without moving the application’s funds during Issue.

A Web transition moves through separate events. Some happen once per relation, some once per thread, and some once for each Strand or each transition. Off-chain work produces keys, signed intents, proofs and an assembly. On-chain work records checked proofs as claim outputs and then settles the transition. Nothing happens automatically: a proof-claim output records checked evidence, moves no application funds, and has no effect until a builder assembles and submits a settlement that the validator accepts.

The invoice route has two onchain transactions

For the one-Strand invoice design: one Issue checks the proof and creates one claim output. One settlement consumes that claim output and the escrow state, burns the claim token, pays the seller and fee recipient, and creates the continuing escrow. Opening the agreement, one-time genesis and any post-expiry claim reclaim are separate transactions. The invoice source has no reserve-return action.

Conditions, Strands, claim outputs and transactions are counted separately. A Web names its required conditions, or roles. A Strand covers one or more of them, since one whole-execution proof can serve several roles. The three invoice roles can therefore be carried by a single Strand.

Each Strand becomes exactly one proof-claim output through its own Issue transaction. n required Strands therefore mean n Issue transactions, n claim outputs and one settlement that consumes them all. One-time genesis and any post-expiry reclaims are extra. These counts are structural readings of source, not measured transaction counts.

Several claim envelopes can exist for one statement digest. Settlement consumes each envelope once, as the ledger spends each output once. Issuing several claims in one transaction is not possible without a change to the claim policy.

The following are paid in ADA, separately from the application amounts: - network fees - collateral - the min-ADA each proof-claim output holds from issuance until settlement or reclaim - the lovelace thread reserve None of these is estimated here. A program fee is paid in the program asset and is neither debt repayment nor a network fee. A reserve duty is a program liability, not the thread reserve.

Owners sign their intents before proving. Each claimant signs the settlement that consumes their claim, and they sign a reclaim after expiry. No published signer matrix covers each transaction. The supplied material does not say who must sign an Issue transaction or genesis, and no genuine intent signature or wallet signing display has been observed. The worksheet's fee-funded 112 CASH escrow and the 25 A / 10 B treasury backing depend on funding actions and consumer-enforced backing policies that the source programs do not contain.

Open composition question from the source review: How does the rule that n required Strands are consumed by one settlement fit with the V2 source note that it 'refuses two claims for one consumed outref'? The supplied text does not resolve this.

Unused claims, stale state and readback

Any catalogue account can Revoke, which advances the sequence, and a competing settlement can spend the same state first. Either event leaves the issued claims pointing at a stale target. Those claims cannot settle and stay locked until the claimant's chosen expiry. Someone can copy a proof and re-issue it with the same claimant and a new expiry, but only that claimant can release it. An envelope with the wrong claimant can be minted but cannot settle.

The claimant releases the output and recovers its min-ADA. Reclaim before expiry is not available. The claimant signs. The supplied sources do not state who funds the reclaim transaction's network fee and collateral.

A Web in which a required condition has no covering Strand has no authority over the contract's effects. Its already-issued claim outputs still exist on-chain, keep holding their min-ADA, and can be released only by their claimant. Issuing a proof claim is not settlement and executes nothing. A partial payment that carries every Strand its step requires is different: it is ordinary financial progress, and its remaining duties continue on the record.

After submission, a timeout means the outcome is unknown, not that the transition did not happen. The application keeps its last observation and submission reference. A missed deadline is never treated as evidence that the transition did not execute.

Statement digest

The claim policy checks the Groth16 proof against the statement digest when it issues the claim. At settlement, the validator recomputes the digest from the transaction and the spent state and requires equality with that issued digest. This comparison is what joins correct computation to a particular ledger step. Settlement does not verify the proof a second time.

Outputs and allocation

The validator checks the actual seller, fee-recipient and beneficiary outputs against the amounts the statement fixes and the owners' signed caps. A profile chooses one of two rules: one output per obligation, or a recomputed amount partition that admits combined payments, with every obligation allocated exactly once. No input value may count toward two obligations. The strict source rule covers redeemers of one script hash only.

Custody and duties

The thread datum carries a complete custody snapshot, and custody must equal it exactly. Duties change only through recorded accruals and discharges carried over from the predecessor state. A thread with a nonzero duty cannot close. Liabilities do not have to be fully backed: the source requires only that custody stay non-negative, and full backing is an application policy that a consumer would have to enforce.

Network and external anchors

The network byte is declared by the catalogue and is not compared with the executing ledger. Cross-network separation therefore rests on outref uniqueness. External facts and reference-input data count only when the validator reads the datum, authenticates its origin and applies a named freshness limit. Being present on the ledger does not establish history, and a validity interval does not date a datum.

The agreement supplies the rules

This is the actual partial-payment action in the supplied invoice source. It requires an open invoice and a positive payment smaller than the outstanding amount. The fee comes before the seller transfer; discharge reduces the invoice, and the postcondition checks the resulting duty.

installment-invoice.mori · partial action
  action partial(amount: UInt) {
    requires opened and amount > 0;
    let opening = liability_amount("invoice");
    requires amount < opening;
    fee(CASH, "escrow", "fees", 2);
    transfer(CASH, "escrow", "seller", amount);
    discharge("invoice", amount);
    next paid = paid + amount;
    ensures liability_amount("invoice") == opening - amount;
  }

The source also preserves marker, keeps opened true and increases paid by the payment. A correct transition accounts for preserved state as well as new payments. The proposed 112-CASH opening is an additional funding policy; this excerpt does not implement it.

05The proof system
Groth16 proofs and trusted setup

Groth16 lets a prover show that a computation satisfies a fixed set of constraints without publishing its private inputs. A Strand uses it to carry a compact proof that the financial calculation follows those rules. Here is what the proof contains, what the validator checks and why setup is needed.

What the verifier actually checks

A Groth16 proof concerns one relation: a fixed system of arithmetic constraints that the compiler produces from one program action. Each value in that system sits on a wire. A few wires are declared public inputs, and the verifier receives their values directly. Every other wire is a witness wire: the prover supplies it and the verifier never receives it. A proof is meant to convince a verifier that the prover knows witness values that, together with the given public inputs, satisfy every constraint. Zero-knowledge means the proof itself adds nothing further about the witness wires. It does not make a value secret when anyone can reconstruct it from public data. A value that is not a public input is therefore not automatically private.

Setup for the relation produces two public keys: a proving key and a verification key. The prover combines the proving key, the public inputs and the witness into a proof of exactly three group elements. That size stays the same however many constraints the relation has. A proof is made for one relation's keys. Checking it under another relation's verification key provides no evidence about that other relation.

Verification takes the verification key, the public inputs and the three proof elements, and evaluates one pairing-product equation. Conceptually, the verifier first folds the public input values into a single group element using points fixed in the verification key. It then uses pairings, which let it compare products of values that stay hidden inside group elements. The assurance is computational and conditional. If the setup was honest and Groth16 knowledge soundness holds, a passing check means that, except with negligible probability, the prover knew a satisfying witness for those public inputs. The recorded test setup does not meet the first condition, because its operator can forge proofs. In the Cardano design, this check runs inside the claim policy when a proof-claim output is issued, not again at settlement.

In a Web relation, the Groth16 public inputs are just two field elements. Together they carry a digest of the statement bytes that the settling validator later rebuilds. The statement bytes are not themselves public inputs. They sit on witness wires, and the circuit hashes them to that digest with a BLAKE2b gadget inside the circuit. Tying the two public inputs to particular statement bytes therefore rests on further assumptions. Among them are BLAKE2b collision resistance and the in-circuit gadget actually matching its specification. Correspondence between the compiled circuit, its verification key and that gadget has not been established. Even when those assumptions hold, the proof establishes only that the computation over those bytes was carried out correctly. It never establishes that the bytes describe the real ledger or a true observation from outside the chain.

Public inputs, statement bytes and the remaining witness

Three kinds of value need to be kept apart. First, the Groth16 public inputs: the two field elements that carry the statement digest. Second, the statement bytes. They describe a single step: the state being spent, its sequence number, the settling script, the catalogue, the signed intents, the claimant and the body values the step computes over. Inside the circuit these bytes are witness bits, which the circuit hashes to the digest. Outside the circuit they are public in practice, because the settling validator and any observer can rebuild them from the transaction and the state it spends. Sitting on a witness wire does not make them private. Third, the remaining witness values, described below.

The remaining witness values are whatever else the prover supplies to satisfy the constraints. Zero-knowledge keeps them out of the proof. They stay hidden only to the extent that they cannot be reconstructed from public data, and zero-knowledge does not make a transition private. The statement bytes, the proof-claim outputs, the settlement transaction and its actual outputs are all visible on the ledger. Public evidence bundles leave out witness values, private keys and setup secrets.

Execution, universal properties and ledger facts

Three kinds of assertion need to be kept apart. A concrete execution proof concerns one transition: this payment, fee and duty update follow from these statement values. A universal property certificate would concern every execution in a specified domain. An authenticated ledger claim concerns a fact reached through a named ledger-authentication path. In the current design, a Strand can only be a concrete execution proof. The other two are not established Strand kinds, and none of the three can stand in for another.

The proof supplies correct computation over the values in the statement, and the validator supplies authenticated premises and scope. Ledger facts come from the transaction itself, not from the proof. These include which state is being spent, which outputs exist and who signed. For each Strand, the settling validator rebuilds the statement from the transaction and the state it spends, then compares it with the digest the claim policy checked at issuance. It does not verify the proof again.

Facts from outside the chain, such as a delivery document, a price or a rate observation, enter only with a named issuer, an authentication path and a freshness limit. Reading a reference input requires the validator to read the datum and authenticate its origin. An input being unspent when the transaction applies shows presence only, not exclusivity or history. A validity interval neither dates nor authenticates a datum.

Why there is a trusted setup

Groth16 needs parameters for the exact compiled circuit. Setup derives the proving and verification keys using secret randomness. If someone retains that randomness, they can forge proofs. A common ceremony divides this work into two phases so part of it can be shared.

Phase 1
Parameters that circuits can share

Often called powers of tau, this phase prepares parameters for a particular curve and capacity. Circuits can reuse them when their curve and format are compatible and they fit within that capacity.

Phase 2
Keys for one compiled circuit

Each distinct circuit needs its own second phase. Its constraints, public-input layout and bounds determine which keys are valid. New values can reuse those keys; a changed circuit needs a new phase 2.

Why changing a payment differs from changing a circuit

Take the partial-payment action of the installment invoice program. Compiling it produces one relation in which predicate_digest, shape_digest and body_len are constants. That relation's phase 2 produces a verification key. The key's digest sits in the catalogue, and owner intents sign it as the target together with the predicate and shape digests.

Now take a 35 CASH partial payment on one escrow and a different partial payment on another escrow, with another claimant and another sequence number. Both use the same relation and the same keys, because only free context and body values differ. Their statements differ, so under BLAKE2b collision resistance their digests differ except with negligible probability. A claim made for one step therefore does not match the statement the validator rebuilds for the other.

The conditional-reserve allocate action belongs to a different program. No compiled relation for it is supplied. If it were compiled under the current path with a different predicate_digest constant, it would be a distinct circuit with its own phase 2. It could still share a phase 1 with the invoice relation if the curve, format and capacity are compatible. Changing a bound or the public-input layout of the invoice relation itself also produces a new circuit and a new verification-key digest. In the reviewed source, catalogues are fixed at genesis, so putting that new key into use would need a key-migration action. That action is a proposal; the reviewed source has no key migration.

Roles and relations are counted separately. The invoice Web names three conditions: payment arithmetic, fee accounting and duty continuation. A single whole-execution proof over the partial-payment action covers all three, so that design needs one relation. Proving the fee or the duty update separately would need a separately compiled relation for each, with its own phase 2. Naming source expressions 'fee' or 'liability' does not create a separate proof producer.

How parameterized reuse fits the current compiler

The unit for counting setups is the distinct compiled relation, including any additional Groth16 wrapper circuit. The number of transactions, wallets, users or claims on Cardano does not set the count, and counts of logical properties or review identifiers do not determine it. New transactions, users and public-input values inside an unchanged compiled relation reuse its keys. Assets are a different case in the current source. The invoice program declares its CASH asset as a literal, so a different asset may mean a different program, a different predicate identity and therefore a different circuit. Whether it does depends on exactly which fields predicate_digest covers, and the source file that defines that coverage was not read. Aggregating proofs does not remove the setups that the underlying circuits need.

In the common two-phase architecture, the exact count is one phase 2 for each distinct compiled circuit in a frozen circuit inventory, plus one phase 1 for each compatible parameter set that inventory uses. As a worked example, K compatible circuits on one curve need one shared phase 1 and K circuit-specific phase 2 stages. That counts stages, not K + 1 complete ceremonies. The figure holds only if every circuit uses the same curve and format and fits the shared phase-1 capacity.

Reusing a parameterized circuit and compiling predicate_digest as a constant can look contradictory, but they operate at different levels. A parameterized relation with fixed bounds covers many claims without a new setup, but only for the values its compiled form leaves free. In the current Rust source those free values are the context and body values, while predicate_digest is fixed inside the constraints. Every distinct predicate identity is therefore a distinct circuit with its own phase 2, even when two predicates look structurally alike.

A future universal circuit that takes the predicate as an input would change this comparison, and so would a target with a universal reference string, such as Midnight's PLONK/KZG infrastructure. Even such a design is still a bounded relation, not one unbounded circuit for every future Cardano application. The 235 pre-dedup review identifiers, the 20 retained families and the seven evidence layers describe review scope, not a setup count. The circuit inventory is not frozen, so no Cardano-wide total can be stated.

Inside the reviewed relation source, some header fields are compiled constants: the tag, the family, predicate_digest, shape_digest and body_len. The context fields from vk_digest through intents_digest, including claimant_vkh, are free witness bits. The circuit hashes these bits into the statement digest but does not interpret them, so it accepts whatever context it is given without checking it. These fields remain part of the publicly rebuildable statement bytes. Calling them witness bits describes how the circuit receives them, not secrecy. Comparing that context with the real transaction is the consuming validator's job, not the circuit's.

When can the same keys be used again?
The amounts or users change

The keys can be reused if the compiled circuit stays the same. New transactions and different public-input values do not, by themselves, require setup again.

The compiled rules change

A circuit with different constraints, bounds or public-input layout needs a new phase 2. A new Groth16 wrapper also has its own circuit. Aggregation does not remove setup for the base circuits.

One bounded circuit covers several kinds of claim

A parameterized circuit can cover many claims within fixed bounds. That is how a catalogue can contain more claim types than circuits. It does not make the circuit unbounded.

What makes a ceremony trustworthy?

Proving and verification keys are public. The secret is the setup randomness, often called toxic waste. Anyone who holds it can produce verifying proofs for statements that have no witness, so it must not survive the setup. A multi-party phase is trustworthy when its transcript verifies and at least one participant in that phase was honest, under the protocol's assumptions. Every phase needs that property.

The recorded setup is a single-party test setup whose operator can forge proofs

Every proof context in those records was supplied by the caller, not taken from a real transaction. No on-chain transaction is recorded.

Both records use a single-party test setup whose operator can forge proofs for any digest and any claimant. No production security claim follows from them. No production ceremony is recorded in the supplied sources, none is proposed here, and the total number of setups required is unknown. The recorded local CPU and memory figures cover the whole compiled Issue evaluation, including its pairing. They are not an isolated verification cost, ledger execution units, a fee or a transaction fit, and they support no claimed cost advantage.

How many setups for all of Cardano?

There is no fixed Cardano-wide total. Count one phase 2 for each distinct compiled circuit, including wrappers. Count phase 1 separately for each compatible parameter set those circuits use; an existing set can be reused if its capacity is sufficient. Claim types and architectural layers do not determine the number of circuits. A total requires a frozen inventory of compiled circuits.

07Questions
Using Webs in an application

Answers about authorization, unused proof claims and transaction costs.

Does a proof claim mint my application’s token?

No. Issuing a proof claim creates an output that records checked evidence. Settlement consumes it and burns the claim token. Your application’s token has its own minting policy, which controls supply, recipients and any issuance cap.

Assumptions and implementation details
↗
Do I need permission from Moriarty to build an application?

No. Developers can write, compile, prove and deploy programs in the supported language without approval from Moriarty maintainers. The application still has to follow the ledger’s rules and respect the authorization of anyone whose assets it moves. A contract may also define access rules for its own users.

Assumptions and implementation details
↗
What does the owner authorize?

The owner signs an intent before proving. It defines per-asset limits on gross debits, fees and new liabilities, as well as the minimum net amount each beneficiary must receive. Its validity window, one-time nonce and exact proof relation limit where and when it can be used. Network fees are shown separately.

Assumptions and implementation details
↗
Who signs the settlement transaction?

Each proof-claim output names a claimant. Consuming it at settlement requires that claimant’s signature and burns the claim token. The validator also checks the prior signed intents of the accounts being debited. Those owner authorizations and the later claimant signatures serve different purposes.

Assumptions and implementation details
↗
How do I know the payment happened?

Check that the transaction was included, wait for the finality your application requires, then read back the actual outputs and remaining obligations. Reconcile those amounts with the owner’s signed intent. A timeout leaves the outcome unknown; it does not establish that the payment failed.

Assumptions and implementation details
↗
Can a partial payment have a complete Web?

Yes. Completeness means every required condition for this payment has proof coverage. It does not mean the entire invoice has been paid. The unpaid invoice balance and reserve obligation remain in the next state.

Assumptions and implementation details
↗
What if the Web never settles?

The issued claim outputs keep holding their minimum ADA. Their claimant can reclaim them after the chosen expiry by signing a reclaim transaction. If a catalogue account advances its sequence through revocation, or a competing settlement spends the same state, the old claims can no longer settle and must wait for expiry.

Assumptions and implementation details
↗
Does splitting a Web into more Strands reduce the cost?

Each Strand needs its own Issue transaction on this route, and its claim output holds minimum ADA until settlement or reclaim. A single execution proof covering several conditions may be simpler. Comparing costs requires measuring complete transactions on the target network; splitting alone does not establish a saving.

Transaction signers and authorization boundaries

What the reviewed V2 source fixes (uncompiled-source reading): owners sign intents before proving; each intent signs per-asset gross_cap, fee_cap, net_floor and liability_cap for a beneficiary, a validity window, nonce = seq and the target predicate, VK and shape digests. Each Issue transaction carries one proof, checked by the C2 claim policy, and mints one claim output. The settlement transaction requires the signature of each claimant whose claim output it consumes and burns that output’s token. Unused claims are reclaimed with the claimant’s signature after the claimant-chosen expiry. A supplied approved=true is consented to by the effect-derived required signers, and by a listed advancer if any; that is the debited account’s consent, not a separate governance decision. The claimant is prover-chosen bytes bound at consumption, not an authenticated prover identity. Open: no transaction-by-transaction signer matrix (intent authorization, each Issue, settlement, reclaim) is published; the supplied material does not state who must sign an Issue transaction; no genuine intent signature is observed in the supplied proof records; no wallet signing display exists.

Acceptance, finality and effect readback

Design: after submission the application separates inclusion, required finality and complete readback of outputs and remaining duties, and reconciles actual per-asset effects against the signed intents; a timeout leaves observation unknown and is not evidence of nonexecution. Status: no transaction build, submit or observe interface exists; no on-chain transaction is recorded; the acceptance and readback signal for a supported target still has to be defined and demonstrated, Preview first and later Preprod.
