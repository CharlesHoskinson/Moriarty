# Layer 4 — Arithmetization

This is an educational translation proposal for Layer 4, not adopted product copy or a claim about an implemented Moriarty circuit. The chapter’s useful core is: take a computation and state the relationships its values must satisfy; represent those relationships as polynomial constraints over a finite field; then let a proof system check that the supplied values satisfy them. The spreadsheet analogy is a starting point, while the chapter itself warns that it does not explain the proof mechanism.

For Webs, explain the boundary in terms of one partial invoice payment. The source fixture starts with 110 CASH, an invoice duty of 100 CASH, and a reserve duty of 10 CASH. At a payment amount of 35 CASH, the source arithmetic gives a 2 CASH program fee, 37 CASH gross escrow debit, 73 CASH closing escrow cash, 65 CASH remaining invoice duty, and 10 CASH remaining reserve duty. The fee does not reduce either duty. If the worksheet instead starts with 112 CASH, its two-CASH extra funding covers this one partial fee and leaves 75 CASH against 75 CASH of duties. The 112 mode is an added funding assumption: S12 has no extra-funding action. With source funding of 110, the arithmetic result has a 2 CASH backing gap. That gap is a separate backing-policy question; it must not be described as a source partial-payment refusal, since the fixture expects amounts 1–99 to satisfy the source partial action’s stated amount rule.

Show how a relation could express the selected source arithmetic, for example `grossDebit = amount + fee`, `cashAfter = cashBefore - grossDebit`, `invoiceDutyAfter = invoiceDutyBefore - amount`, `reserveDutyAfter = reserveDutyBefore`, `sellerReceipt = amount`, and `feeRecipientReceipt = fee`. A proof relation would need to bind the appropriate values to its statement and constrain all required checks. These equations are a teaching sketch from the fixture, not a recovered circuit, compiled relation, or implementation claim. Field equations alone describe arithmetic modulo the field; they do not by themselves establish that a value is a nonnegative CASH amount in the intended integer range. Range constraints and a justified integer encoding are needed wherever the financial rule depends on bounded integer meanings. A backing condition such as `cashAfter >= invoiceDutyAfter + reserveDutyAfter` also needs its own correctly encoded comparison/range logic. Keep that condition separate from the source action’s partial-payment predicate.

The webpage should keep four ideas distinct: the financial rule being described; the mathematical constraints chosen to encode it; authenticated values and state supplied by the transaction/validator; and the proof system that checks a constraint relation. The V2 copy says proof claims do not move application funds and that the validator rebuilds statements from the transaction; the Layer 4 proposal should point to that division without implying that an arithmetic proof authenticates ledger facts, a signature, delivery, or settlement. Do not imply that one Strand maps to one circuit or that this example establishes a complete Web implementation.

## Proposed reader path

1. Begin with “What must stay true?” and use the seller payment, separate fee, and two continuing duties as the familiar financial rule.
2. Reveal the arithmetic rows and then the corresponding constraint sketch. Let the reader change the partial amount within 1–99; show which values change and which duty stays at 10.
3. Offer a source-funding/112-funding comparison. Explain that the first displays a two-CASH backing gap while the second assumes one extra two-CASH allowance; neither slider state is a proof or a ledger transition.
4. Open a compact “why ranges matter” panel: a finite-field value wraps modulo a prime, whereas CASH rules use bounded integer amounts. The design must show a range constraint as an additional requirement, without teaching a particular encoding as if it were Moriarty’s circuit.
5. Close with a boundary card: satisfying arithmetic constraints would establish only the relation over the supplied statement values. The consumer’s transaction and validator provide their own premises and acceptance rules.

## Source mismatch and review questions

The supplied Layer 1 overview (B08, lines 272–298) says the 4×4 Sudoku becomes “72 polynomial constraints.” The exact Layer 4 chapter’s running example counts 16 range constraints, 8 clue constraints, and 72 pairwise uniqueness checks (96 high-level checks); it then estimates roughly 130 R1CS rows after decomposition, while saying CCS can express the 96 directly. The overview appears to count only pairwise uniqueness checks while describing them as the total. Preserve this discrepancy for Opus’s book/copy review rather than silently correcting the book.

Ask Opus to verify before adoption: the chapter’s constraint counts and distinction between a high-level inequality and its auxiliary inverse encoding; any proposed wording about finite-field equations versus integer amounts; whether “a proof does not reveal anything about the witness” needs qualification for the exact system and public statement; all claims about Moriarty’s circuit, Web, Strand, or proof boundary; and the broad chapter claims about current systems, field choices, overhead, or CCS convergence. The supplied Groth paper is evidence about its own arithmetic-circuit satisfiability construction; it does not establish which arithmetization Moriarty or a particular Web uses.
