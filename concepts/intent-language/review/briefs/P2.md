
## YOUR LENS: P2 — expressiveness and the category mapping

You own the claim that this language can actually say what finance needs to say. Test it by writing programs, not by reading prose.

- **Write MIL/1 for hard cases.** Pick at least five and write them in the surface syntax of DESIGN.md §10, as concretely as the design allows: (a) a concentrated-liquidity swap with a tick range; (b) an over-collateralised CDP mint with a liquidation path; (c) a perpetual with periodic funding and a maintenance-margin call; (d) a cross-domain burn-mint bridge transfer with a dispute window; (e) an ERC-4626-style vault deposit and redeem including the empty-pool bootstrap; (f) a batch auction clearing three participants; (g) a flash loan. Where the language cannot express one, say exactly which construct is missing and whether the design's own "open" list already admits it.
- **Audit the category map.** `CATEGORY-MAP.md` claims a verdict per requirement per category. Check the ones that matter. Are any "Expressible" verdicts wrong — that is, does the claimed carrier not actually carry it? Are any "Library" verdicts hiding a needed core mechanism? Are any "Open" items actually expressible, making the design too pessimistic?
- **The five declared-open items** are n-party clearing, flash loans, a liquidation latency bound, competing-slash ordering, and the general form of policy state over reachability. Are these the right five? Is each genuinely open, or is one of them a design decision masquerading as an open question? More importantly: **what is missing from that list** — find the open items the design has not noticed.
- **The asset taxonomy.** Twelve categories reduced to three rules (identity carries domain/issuer/representation; one conservation equation per (domain,asset); `issue` for units and consent for liabilities). Does the taxonomy cover the space? Test it against rebasing tokens, fee-on-transfer tokens, tokens with blacklists or pausable transfers, multi-collateral claims, NFT fractionalisation, and a claim whose obligor is off-chain. Where does the three-rule reduction fail?
- **Solver degrees of freedom.** Holes are the declared search space. Are the holes in the examples the right shape for a solver to actually search? Is there a class of routing or matching problem that cannot be expressed as a hole under the monotonicity constraint — and if so, is that a safety feature or a fatal limitation?
- **Comparison.** Marlowe, Daml/DAML contracts, Anoma intents, ACTUS contract types, the defiformal kernel *[df]*, and ERC-4337/x402-style intent systems all solved adjacent problems. Where does MIL/1 reinvent something worse, and where is it genuinely better? Be specific; one sharp comparison beats a survey.

Your review is strongest if it contains actual MIL/1 code and a case the language cannot express.
