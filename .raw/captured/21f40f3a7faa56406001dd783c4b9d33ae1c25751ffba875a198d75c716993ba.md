# ERC-4626 and Collateral Vaults: A Research Taxonomy and Technical Atlas

## Executive research map

The most important conclusion is that **ERC-4626 is an interface and accounting standard for tokenized claims on managed ERC-20 assets; it is not a collateral-risk standard**. A conforming ERC-4626 component exposes an ERC-20 share token plus standardized asset/share conversion, preview, limit, deposit, mint, withdraw, and redeem semantics. It does not specify whether the underlying strategy is solvent, whether `totalAssets()` represents realizable liquidation value, whether shares are safe to price using `convertToAssets()`, whether withdrawals will remain liquid under stress, or what happens when those shares secure debt somewhere else. citeturn16view0turn16view1turn16view2turn16view3

That boundary explains much of the subsequent literature. The original standard intentionally permits broad implementation freedom. `totalAssets()` is the amount of assets “managed” by the vault; `convertToShares()` and `convertToAssets()` describe an idealized, caller-independent exchange; previews incorporate fees and current conditions; and `max*` methods communicate current limits. These distinctions are extremely useful for composability, but they do not turn an accounting exchange rate into an adversarially robust oracle. citeturn16view0turn16view1

The major security fault line is therefore:

> **share accounting → share valuation → collateral valuation → borrowing power → liquidation proceeds**

A vault can be internally correct at the first step and unsafe at the later ones. Euler's exchange-rate analysis explicitly separates first-deposit manipulation from the more consequential case where a share token is used as lending collateral and an artificially increased share price permits excess borrowing. OpenZeppelin independently describes the direct-donation mechanism: assets enter the vault without corresponding share creation, increasing an internal assets-per-share ratio. citeturn23view0turn23view1

This is not merely theoretical. The 2025 Venus zkSync wUSDM postmortem reports exchange-rate manipulation that left $902,000 of bad debt before recoveries; the June 2025 Resupply incident used a newly deployed, extremely low-liquidity vault and donation/exchange-rate arithmetic to extract roughly $9.5–$9.8 million according to different security analyses. These incidents should not all be labeled “ERC-4626 bugs”: the reusable result is the **mechanism**—a downstream system trusted a manipulable accounting quantity as economic value. citeturn23view10turn24search2turn24search10

The same point extends beyond ERC-4626. The March 15, 2026 Venus THE incident was not an ERC-4626 failure, but BlockSec's analysis shows a related accounting-boundary mistake: direct token donations bypassed intended supply-cap accounting, expanded recognized collateral exposure to 3.67 times the intended limit, and preceded roughly $14.9 million in borrowing and approximately $2.15 million in residual bad debt. It belongs in the taxonomy as an **economic analogue**, not as evidence against ERC-4626 itself. citeturn23view9turn24view8

The standards family has meanwhile expanded in useful but deliberately narrow ways. ERC-7540, now Final, defines asynchronous request/claim flows; ERC-7575, also Final, allows multiple asset entry points and an external share token; ERC-7535 covers native assets. ERC-5143's standardized slippage overloads remain Stagnant; ERC-7887's cancellation extension is Draft; and the July 2026 ERC-8330 NAV snapshot proposal was in Review as of September 8, 2026. None of these proposals, including the NAV proposal, purports to solve collateral safety automatically. ERC-8330 is particularly explicit that standardized NAV publication does not calculate NAV, prove backing, or guarantee that reported NAV is an executable market or redemption price. citeturn17view0turn18view0turn18view1turn18view2turn18view3turn18view4

The evidence base separates naturally into four layers:

| Evidence layer | Strongest current conclusions |
|---|---|
| **Normative standards** | Exact interface, rounding, preview, limit, request, and share-token obligations are well specified. citeturn16view0turn16view2turn17view0turn18view0 |
| **Executable engineering evidence** | Property testing can detect many interface-conformance and rounding problems; major implementations publish substantial tests, audits, and in some cases formal-verification artifacts. It does **not** establish arbitrary strategy or collateral-oracle safety. citeturn23view2turn23view3turn24view0turn24view1 |
| **Empirical economic evidence** | Historical studies establish that liquidations are highly sensitive to price, liquidity, incentives, and system design; overcollateralization is not sufficient by itself to eliminate loss or destabilizing feedback. citeturn20search1turn19search0turn21search2 |
| **Emerging vault-credit research** | 2025–2026 work is beginning to model curator concentration, vault-specific credit risk, recursive leverage, and OEV-aware liquidation, but important results remain preprints and should not yet be treated as settled doctrine. citeturn19academia11turn19academia12turn19academia13turn20search8 |

The central unresolved problem is consequently **compositional security**: given a vault whose interface and local accounting are correct, under what explicit assumptions may another protocol safely treat its shares as collateral? Existing standards expose useful quantities, implementations provide increasingly strong local testing, and lending literature describes liquidation behavior, but there is not yet a generally accepted specification linking those layers.

## Taxonomy, vocabulary, and standards lineage

A useful taxonomy cannot put every object called a “vault” into the same branch. ERC-4626 vault shares, Maker/Sky Vaults, Liquity Troves, Euler credit vaults, Aave lending positions, Centrifuge asynchronous investment vaults, and Enzyme-managed portfolios differ in who owns assets, what the tokenized claim represents, where liabilities reside, and who absorbs losses. Sky's Vault/CDP manager, for example, manages individual collateralized debt positions, while Compound III maintains account collateral internally against base-asset borrowing; neither should be relabeled ERC-4626 merely because “vault” is economically evocative. citeturn15search5turn15search4turn15search9

**Economic architecture taxonomy**

| ID | Architecture | Claim and liability structure | Representative systems |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Tokenized asset-management / yield vault | Depositor receives fungible shares on managed assets. The ERC-4626 layer need not itself create borrower debt. | OpenZeppelin base ERC-4626, Yearn V3, Aave Earn Vaults, Morpho Vault V2, Silo Vaults. Aave's current Earn product explicitly exposes ERC-4626 shares while routing underlying assets into Aave markets. citeturn22search0turn23search0turn14search2 |
| **B** | Collateralized debt position | User owns a position containing collateral and owes protocol/stablecoin debt; the position is not necessarily a fungible pooled share. | Sky Vaults/CDPs and Liquity Troves. citeturn15search5turn14search0 |
| **C** | Lending / credit vault | Depositors own a claim on a pool whose assets fund borrower liabilities; bad debt can impair the pool. | Euler Vault Kit and allocation vaults feeding lending markets. Euler's repository includes dedicated audits, Certora artifacts, source and tests. citeturn24view0 |
| **D** | Vault shares used as collateral | Debt exists in a second system, while the collateral is itself a tokenized claim on a first system. | ERC-4626 shares accepted by lending markets; Venus wUSDM-style integration failures illustrate the key oracle risk. citeturn23view0turn23view10 |
| **E** | Shared-collateral / margin architecture | Account-level collateral supports borrowing or other obligations, often through internal accounting rather than one ERC-20 vault share. | Euler's EVC architecture; Aave and Compound account collateral. Compound III records collateral balances inside Comet. citeturn15search9turn15search11 |
| **F** | Staking/restaking/security collateral | Receipt represents capital exposed to validator or security-system obligations, including possible slashing; exits can be delayed. | Liquid-staking tokens are an explicit motivating asynchronous-redemption example in ERC-7540. citeturn17view0 |
| **G** | Asynchronous / permissioned / RWA vault | Investment claim may involve request queues, issuer processing, valuation dates, custodians, or off-chain settlement. | ERC-7540 and Centrifuge. Centrifuge currently exposes request-based investment and redemption flows and manager-mediated fulfillment. citeturn24search1turn24search3turn24search7 |
| **H** | Structured / multi-asset / nested vault | A common claim spans several asset entry points, strategies, tranches, or downstream vaults. | ERC-7575 and multi-strategy allocators; Aave's current stable-vault documentation describes allocation among approved ERC-4626 strategies. citeturn18view0turn23search1turn23search5 |

The proposed research-topic taxonomy T01–T11 survives the literature review with no need for a fundamental rewrite. The important correction is that these should be **facets**, not subclasses. An Euler EVault, for example, simultaneously belongs under standards/interoperability, lending, valuation, liquidation, governance, and verification; a Centrifuge vault simultaneously belongs under standards, settlement, permissions, valuation, and external dependencies. citeturn24view0turn24search1turn24search11

|---|---|---|---|
| **T01** | Standards and interoperability | Interfaces, extensions, adapters and detection | How much risk metadata can be standardized without embedding policy? |
| **T02** | Asset/share accounting | `totalAssets`, supply, conversions, fees, rounding | Which invariants survive losses, debt and asynchronous settlement? |
| **T03** | Valuation and collateral oracles | Accounting value → economic/NAV/collateral value | What is a manipulation-resistant *realizable* price for a vault share? |

| **T11** | Empirical market structure | Liquidators, utilization, concentration, on-chain behavior | What reproducible longitudinal datasets should become research infrastructure? |

**Standards lineage and current status**

ERC-4626 was created on December 22, 2021 and is Final. It requires an ERC-20 representation of vault shares, even though share transfers may be disabled by reverting on `transfer`/`transferFrom`. It defines one underlying ERC-20 `asset()` for accounting, deposits and withdrawals. citeturn16view0

ERC-5143, created June 9, 2022, attempted to standardize explicit slippage-bounded overloads—such as minimum shares for a deposit and maximum assets for a mint—but it is currently Stagnant. This is historically important because it exposes a conceptual gap in core ERC-4626: previews provide information, but the core transaction signature does not itself carry the user's acceptable execution bound. citeturn18view1

ERC-7535, created October 12, 2023 and now Final, adapts the same conceptual interface to native ETH/native assets. It overrides `asset`, `deposit`, and `mint`, including payable behavior and a native-asset sentinel address; it explicitly does not apply when the underlying is wrapped ETH as an ERC-20. citeturn18view2

ERC-7540, created October 18, 2023 and now Final, is the major semantic extension for delayed settlement. An implementation may make deposits asynchronous, redemptions asynchronous, or both. Requests proceed through Pending → Claimable → Claimed states, and the relevant preview functions must revert because the exchange rate at the later claim may not be known when the request is made. citeturn17view0

ERC-7575, created December 11, 2023 and now Final, separates the vault entry-point contract from the common ERC-20 share representation and allows multiple entry points denominated in different assets. That makes it substantially better suited to multi-asset systems, but it deliberately says nothing about how heterogeneous assets receive a common trustworthy economic valuation. citeturn18view0

ERC-7887, created February 18, 2025, remains Draft. It adds a second asynchronous lifecycle for cancellation because an ERC-7540 request may remain Pending for a long time; while cancellation is pending, corresponding new requests are blocked. citeturn18view3

ERC-8330, created July 5, 2026, was in Review on September 8, 2026. It is not an ERC-4626 extension, but it is highly relevant to RWA and collateral-vault research because it standardizes provider-attributed, timestamped NAV snapshots, corrections, invalidation, staleness semantics and optional aggregation. Its security section and abstract draw the critical line: a NAV observation is not necessarily an executable sale or redemption price. citeturn18view4

**ERC-4626 normative requirements matrix**

| Surface | Normative meaning | What an integrator must *not* infer |
|---|---|---|
| `asset()` | Must identify the ERC-20 underlying used for accounting/deposit/withdrawal and must not revert. citeturn16view0 | That every economic asset held downstream is identical to this token. |
| `totalAssets()` | Reports total underlying assets “managed” by the vault, should include compounding yield, must include fees charged against vault assets, and must not revert. citeturn16view0 | That all reported assets are instantly liquid, mark-to-market correct, or recoverable under stress. |
| `convertToShares()` / `convertToAssets()` | Ideal-scenario, caller-independent conversions; exclude fees/slippage, generally cannot revert, and round down. citeturn16view0turn16view1 | That the returned number is a collateral oracle or guaranteed execution price. |
| `maxDeposit()` / `maxMint()` | Must account for applicable global/user limits, return zero if disabled, and conservatively not exceed the true accepted amount. citeturn16view0turn16view2 | That maximum capacity equals underlying market liquidity after execution. |
| `previewDeposit()` | Includes deposit fees, ignores user/global limit checks, and must not return more shares than a same-transaction `deposit` ultimately returns. citeturn16view0 | That it remains valid after intervening state changes. |
| `previewMint()` | Includes deposit fees and must not underestimate the assets required by a same-transaction mint. citeturn16view2 | A future mint quote. |
| `previewWithdraw()` | Includes withdrawal fees and must not underestimate shares burned by a same-transaction withdrawal. citeturn16view3 | Guaranteed withdrawal liquidity. |
| Entry/exit calls | `deposit`/`mint` and `withdraw`/`redeem` have exact-quantity semantics, emit standardized events and enforce relevant asset/share authorization. citeturn16view2turn16view3 | Solvency, fair NAV, strategy correctness, or collateral fitness. |

Three small examples expose the key conceptual boundaries.

**Empty-vault inflation.** Suppose an unprotected vault begins empty. An attacker deposits 1 asset and receives 1 share, then transfers 100 assets directly to the vault. The apparent ratio is now 101 assets per share. A victim depositing 100 assets can receive `floor(100/101) = 0` shares in a naïve implementation. After the victim's assets enter, the lone attacker share can represent all 201 assets. This is the canonical mechanism behind the first-deposit/inflation literature. OpenZeppelin's implementation and documentation now incorporate virtual assets/shares as a defense, while explicitly warning about nearly empty vaults. citeturn22search0turn23view0

**Collateral-oracle mismatch.** Suppose a lender values a vault share solely through its internal assets-per-share conversion. A direct 100-asset transfer to a vault with 100 assets and 100 shares doubles the accounting ratio from 1 to 2 without minting any new shares. That does **not** automatically make an attacker profitable—the donation itself costs capital—but it demonstrates why an external lender needs a manipulation and realizability model rather than blindly equating internal accounting with collateral value. citeturn23view1turn23view0

**Asynchronous settlement.** In an ERC-7540 asynchronous deposit vault, 100 assets may be requested at time \(t_0\), remain Pending, become Claimable after manager processing, and only then be turned into shares. The standard permits the eventual exchange rate to differ from `convertToShares(100)` at request time and requires `previewDeposit`/`previewMint` to revert for that asynchronous path. An atomic ERC-4626 mental model is therefore wrong for these claims. citeturn17view0

A useful security taxonomy follows the **mechanism**, not the protocol label:

| Mechanism | Preconditions | Failure path | Defenses and their boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Initial-deposit / inflation** | Very low supply; attacker can alter assets before victim share mint | Victim's asset→share conversion rounds against victim | Seed liquidity, virtual assets/shares, higher share precision and explicit slippage checks. These address depositor minting economics but do not automatically make shares safe lending collateral. citeturn22search0turn22search12 |
| **Direct donation / exchange-rate manipulation** | `totalAssets` or equivalent recognizes donated balance; downstream system values shares from that ratio | No new shares are issued, so accounting value per share jumps | Internal accounting, bounded-rate oracles, manipulation-resistant valuation, caps and time-based constraints can help; no single mitigation is universal. citeturn23view0turn23view1 |
| **Rounding accumulation / “stealth” donation** | Integer conversion repeatedly leaves residual value in vault | Small residuals compound into exchange-rate movement | Precision and invariant tests; analyze repeated operations rather than one transaction. The a16z suite is particularly useful for round-trip properties but deliberately stops short of strategy economics. citeturn23view3 |
| **Preview/execution mismatch** | State changes, fees, implementation error, or async semantics | Integrator assumes preview is guaranteed execution | Same-transaction bounds, slippage constraints, correct use of `max*`; in ERC-7540, affected previews intentionally revert. citeturn16view0turn17view0 |
| **Oracle manipulation** | Lender converts internal vault accounting into borrowing power without sufficiently independent market/redeemability checks | Excess debt is created against inflated collateral | Oracle diversity, rate-of-change constraints, liquidity-aware haircuts, manipulation cost analysis. Oracle research emphasizes explicit trust models rather than “price feed” as an undifferentiated abstraction. citeturn21search1turn20academia23 |
| **Liquidity / queue failure** | Assets are deployed, locked, off-chain, or demand for withdrawal spikes | Share remains “valuable” in accounting while redemption is delayed or impaired | Redemption queues, conservative max functions, explicit async states and stress discounts. ERC-7540 standardizes state, not fulfillment guarantees. citeturn17view0turn24search7 |
| **Strategy/adapter loss** | Downstream position loses value or becomes unavailable | `totalAssets` falls or becomes stale/inaccurate; nested losses propagate | Adapter caps, diversification, reporting discipline and explicit loss recognition. Multi-strategy architecture adds dependency risk rather than removing it. citeturn23search1turn24view1 |
| **Privileged-role / upgrade failure** | Manager, curator, owner, guardian or governance can change economically material parameters | Risk profile changes after collateral acceptance | Timelocks, role separation, immutable bounds, monitoring and conservative lender assumptions. Current modular vault designs make these roles an explicit part of system architecture. citeturn15search10turn24view1 |
| **Balance/cap accounting bypass** | Risk cap reads a protocol-specific accounting variable while direct transfers alter economically relevant balances another way | Actual exposure exceeds intended cap | Define caps over the same state used for solvency and test direct-transfer paths. The 2026 Venus THE incident is a strong non-ERC-4626 analogue. citeturn23view9 |

The mitigation literature therefore needs two separate questions that are often conflated:

**Does it protect a depositor entering the vault?** Virtual shares, additional share precision, seed deposits, routers, and explicit min/max outputs can make first-deposit theft or ordinary slippage harder. citeturn22search0turn18view1

**Does it protect a lender accepting the share as collateral?** That requires a second analysis involving donation recoverability, external market depth, redemption liquidity, strategy loss, oracle update policy, rate-of-change limits, share concentration and liquidation proceeds. Euler's 2024 analysis explicitly reaches the conclusion that no single exchange-rate mitigation fits every situation. citeturn23view0

The multi-victim edge case is particularly instructive. OpenZeppelin's virtual-assets/share construction economically penalizes the canonical donation attacker, but a public 2024 issue constructs a scenario where an attacker can recover profitability across repeated victim deposits when the decimal offset is zero. That issue should not be read as invalidating virtual shares wholesale; it demonstrates why a security statement must name its threat model and quantifiers—one victim versus many, one operation versus repeated operations, local depositor safety versus downstream collateral safety. citeturn22search12

Liquidation architecture produces another major fork in the taxonomy. Sky's Liquidation 2.0 seizes unsafe Vault collateral and debt and uses Dutch auctions designed for immediate settlement; Liquity V2 first uses per-market Stability Pools, then JIT liquidation and finally borrower redistribution when necessary; Compound III's `absorb` uses protocol base-asset reserves to settle an underwater account and moves collateral to the protocol, which can later sell that collateral when reserves are below target. These mechanisms allocate latency, market-depth, keeper and bad-debt risk very differently. citeturn15search0turn14search0turn15search1

Liquity's own 2026 risk disclosure is a useful reminder that isolation is not absolute economic insulation: it describes branch shutdown thresholds and acknowledges that severe collateral failure can still produce unbacked BOLD and confidence-driven redemption stress. That is exactly the level at which “vault solvency” must expand from a local collateral ratio into system-wide asset, liability and liquidity analysis. citeturn14search5

## Literature and technical-writing atlas

The scholarly corpus is much broader than papers containing “ERC-4626” in their titles. The most useful foundational papers predate the standard because they establish models for lending pools, leverage, liquidation, oracle trust, and collateralized stablecoin instability—the economic substrate into which ERC-4626 shares are now composed.

**Core scholarly literature**

| Source | Status and contribution | Why it matters for vault research |
|---|---|---|
| **Bartoletti, Chiang & Lluch-Lafuente, “SoK: Lending Pools in Decentralized Finance”** | Formal archetypal model of lending pools, designed to abstract common behavior from implementation detail and establish general properties/vulnerabilities. The work originated in 2020 and entered the FC 2021 literature. citeturn20search0turn20search2 | Best starting point for separating pooled assets, borrower liabilities and protocol invariants from any particular ERC interface. |
| **Gudgeon et al., “DeFi Protocols for Loanable Funds”** | 2020 AFT-era analysis of Compound, Aave and dYdX interest-rate mechanisms, liquidity and cross-protocol market efficiency. citeturn20academia22 | Establishes that the economics of a lending “vault” depend on utilization and inter-market behavior, not share accounting alone. |
| **Perez et al., “Liquidations: DeFi on a Knife-edge”** | Historical Compound empirical study through September 2020; quantified liquidatable exposure and liquidator-response behavior. citeturn20search1turn20search3 | Foundational empirical evidence that collateral safety is jointly an oracle, incentive, liquidity and execution problem. |
| **Qin et al., “An Empirical Study of DeFi Liquidations”** | Peer-reviewed IMC 2021 study spanning Aave, Compound, MakerDAO and dYdX. citeturn19search0turn20search5 | Broadens liquidation evidence beyond one protocol and links incentives to realized collateral sales. |
| **Werner et al., “SoK: Decentralized Finance”** | Broad systematization of DeFi and its technical/economic security categories. citeturn20search6 | Useful framing for keeping contract correctness separate from economic security. |
| **Eskandari et al., “SoK: Oracles from the Ground Truth to Market Manipulation”** | Peer-reviewed AFT 2021 oracle taxonomy; analyzes trust models, attack surfaces and mitigations. citeturn21search1turn21search4 | Essential prerequisite before treating any vault exchange rate as a price oracle. |
| **Klages-Mundt & Minca, “While Stability Lasts”** | Formal stochastic model, subsequently published in *Mathematical Finance*, that derives stable/unstable regimes and deleveraging spirals for non-custodial stablecoins. citeturn21search2turn21search5 | Strong theoretical answer to the mistaken intuition that overcollateralization alone eliminates systemic risk. |
| **Wang et al., “Speculative Multipliers on DeFi”** | FC 2022 work on quantifying on-chain leverage risks. citeturn21search0 | Directly relevant when ERC-4626/LST shares are recursively deposited and borrowed against. |

| **Baude, Danos & El Khalloufi, “Leveraged positions on decentralized lending platforms”** | January 2026 preprint deriving optimized recursive leveraged positions under several interest-rate models and performing historical backtesting. citeturn20search8 | Useful current mathematical treatment of leverage loops; still preprint evidence. |
| **Sadeghi & Feinstein, “Liquidation Dynamics in DeFi and the Role of Transaction Fees”** | February 2026 preprint modeling liquidation and OEV with a CPMM oracle and proving fee-dependent manipulation bounds within that model. citeturn19academia11 | Important emerging bridge between liquidation design, MEV and oracle economics; do not generalize its theorem outside its assumptions. |
| **Zbandut & Goldstein, “Institutionalizing risk curation in decentralized credit”** | December 2025 preprint arguing, with empirical measurements, that ERC-4626 curator layers have become an important locus of underwriting and concentration. citeturn19academia12 | Directly addresses the governance transition from protocol-level asset listing toward permissionless managed vaults. |
| **Zbandut & Goldstein, “Vault as a credit instrument”** | April 2026 preprint proposing a three-level risk decomposition—mechanical loss channels, governance and code integrity—and measurable vault-credit metrics. citeturn19academia13 | Closest recent paper to a generalized collateral-vault risk framework; presently a proposal rather than established consensus. |
| **“An Analysis of Fixed-Spread Liquidation Lending in DeFi”** | 2024 peer-reviewed proceedings work modeling fixed-spread liquidation. citeturn19search2 | Useful counterpoint to auction and Stability-Pool architectures. |

A notable pattern emerges. **The mature literature is strongest on generic lending, liquidation, stablecoin dynamics and oracles; the ERC-4626-specific scholarly literature remains comparatively young.** ERC-4626's most practically important security knowledge has therefore appeared first in standards discussions, open-source implementations, audits, security blogs and incident analyses rather than in conventional academic venues. That conclusion is an inference from the corpus above and the much richer ERC-4626-specific engineering literature below. citeturn23view0turn23view1turn23view2turn22search0

The strongest methodological lesson from these technical sources is that **a vault integration should be specified in layers**:

\[
\text{interface correctness}
\;\not\Rightarrow\;
\text{accounting correctness}
\;\not\Rightarrow\;
\text{manipulation-resistant valuation}
\;\not\Rightarrow\;
\text{liquid collateral}
\;\not\Rightarrow\;
\text{system solvency}.
\]

Every implication in that chain requires additional assumptions. ERC-4626 establishes much of the first layer; property tests probe that layer; donation research attacks the accounting→valuation boundary; oracle research studies valuation robustness; and liquidation/stablecoin research studies the final transition from marked collateral value to debt recovery. citeturn16view0turn23view3turn23view1turn21search1turn19search0

Eight systems warrant deeper architectural inspection.

**OpenZeppelin ERC-4626.** The principal state transition is asset transfer in → share mint and share burn → asset transfer out. The accounting boundary is the relationship between total share supply and `totalAssets()`. Its major security contribution is the configurable virtual-assets/share construction introduced to make an empty-vault inflation attack more expensive; current source also warns integrators to enforce acceptable received amounts. The trust boundary is intentionally small because this is a base class, but that also means strategy, permissions and downstream valuation remain the derived contract's responsibility. citeturn22search0

**Yearn V3.** Yearn is better understood as an asset-management architecture than a generic vault library. Its current repositories expose both the V3 vault system and a separate tokenized-strategy implementation, with explicit technical specification files and testing configurations. The important research boundary is *reporting*: share accounting must turn potentially complicated strategy positions into a coherent vault asset value. A loss or stale/misreported strategy valuation reaches share holders through that reporting boundary. citeturn24view2turn24view4

**Morpho Vault V2.** The repository is architected around a vault plus adapters/allocations and contains dedicated audit and Certora directories. This is the canonical modern example of **risk curation above permissionless markets**: depositors own vault shares, while curatorial/allocator logic chooses where capital is deployed and what exposure constraints apply. The depositor therefore inherits not just lending-market risk but curation, adapter, liquidity and governance risk. That structural observation is consistent with the 2025 curator-layer research, although the paper's broader empirical claims remain preprint findings. citeturn24view1turn19academia12

**Euler Vault Kit and Ethereum Vault Connector.** EVK is unusually important because it crosses the line from yield-vault accounting into explicit credit. The repository contains source, tests, audits and Certora verification material, and external audits have separately reviewed both the vault kit and its oracle infrastructure. Conceptually, a liability vault identifies acceptable collateral while the connector mediates account/collateral relationships and authorization. A loss can therefore arise from depositor accounting, borrower default, collateral valuation, liquidation, connector authorization, or governance configuration; “ERC-4626 compliant” addresses only part of that graph. citeturn24view0turn7search5turn7search7

**Centrifuge.** Centrifuge is the clearest representative of asynchronous/RWA semantics. Its current documentation distinguishes an `AsyncRequestManager` for ERC-7540 operations from synchronous management, and its investor flow separates request, issuer processing and claim. Current product documentation also allows synchronous deposits with asynchronous redemptions and uses action-specific compliance hooks. Consequently the accounting state is not merely “assets versus shares”: Pending and Claimable claims, issuer action, valuation cadence, compliance eligibility and off-chain settlement become first-class state variables. citeturn24search1turn24search3turn24search11

**Silo Vaults.** Silo's 2026 documentation describes ERC-4626 vaults managed by third parties that allocate deposits across Silo markets. Its oracle architecture separately supports borrowing-power and liquidation/solvency configurations, including ERC-4626-based pricing adapters. The useful research insight is that risk isolation at the underlying lending-market level does not make a multi-market allocator risk-free: an allocator can deliberately aggregate exposure across several otherwise isolated markets. citeturn14search2turn14search4

**Liquity V2.** A Trove is a borrower position, not a depositor share. The borrower selects an interest rate and supplies one of the supported collateral classes; when the position becomes unsafe, the market's Stability Pool is first-line liquidation liquidity, followed by JIT liquidation and then debt/collateral redistribution if necessary. Liquity's own risk documentation explicitly models branch shutdown and residual bad-debt states. This makes it an excellent control case for studying collateral vaults without importing ERC-4626 vocabulary where it does not belong. citeturn14search0turn14search5

**Sky/Maker.** Sky's CDP Manager gives users transferable/manageable individual Vault positions; unsafe positions enter Liquidation 2.0, where collateral and debt move to protocol accounting and collateral is sold through Dutch auctions. The loss path therefore runs borrower collateral → protocol bad-debt accounting → auction execution → any residual system debt, which is structurally different from a tokenized yield vault whose loss appears directly as falling assets per share. citeturn15search5turn15search0turn15search3

Across all eight systems, the most reusable software idea is not one contract implementation but **explicit boundary decomposition**:

```text
asset custody
    ↓
position / strategy accounting
    ↓
share or account claim
    ↓
valuation / oracle
    ↓
borrowing-power rule
    ↓
liquidation eligibility
    ↓
liquidation execution
    ↓
bad-debt / loss allocation
```

An audit or proof that stops before the next arrow cannot establish properties after that arrow. This is why the a16z conformance suite's explicit refusal to test share-price/yield implementation details is a virtue rather than a defect: its scope is auditable. citeturn23view3

| Reader | Sequence | Expected outcome |

A practical reproduction program should start with **local state machines, not live protocols**. Instantiate a minimal ERC-4626 vault; run deposit/mint/withdraw/redeem round-trip properties; add an attacker that can transfer assets directly; vary initial supply and decimal offset; then place the share behind a toy lending market that accepts `convertToAssets()` as an oracle. The next stage should add bounded-rate pricing, redemption liquidity and liquidation. This progression isolates exactly which property fails at each composition boundary and aligns with the scope distinction in the generalized test suite. citeturn23view3turn23view0

For asynchronous research, a useful state machine is:

```text
                           ┌──────── cancellation request ────────┐
                           │                                      ↓
assets/shares → Pending → Claimable → Claimed             Cancel Pending
                 │            │                                ↓
                 │            └── exchange rate may differ   Cancel Claimable
                 │                                             ↓
                 └──── fulfillment policy outside          Cancellation Claimed
                       the core interface
```

ERC-7540 standardizes the main Pending → Claimable → Claimed path and requires the user/controller to pull the result rather than having the vault push it; ERC-7887's Draft adds the cancellation lifecycle. citeturn17view0turn18view3

The deeper collateral-design research should then replace “share price” with four separate variables:

\[
V_{\text{accounting}},
\qquad
V_{\text{redemption}},
\qquad
V_{\text{market}},
\qquad
V_{\text{stressed liquidation}}.
\]

ERC-4626 gives useful information about the first two under a particular vault's rules, but it does not assert equality among all four. ERC-8330 independently reinforces the same conceptual distinction for NAV by stating that a published NAV is not necessarily executable. citeturn16view0turn18view4

That separation should be the organizing principle for future collateral-vault research.

## Reusable artifacts, research method, and coverage

The research corpus was assembled in multiple passes: normative Ethereum standards first; then primary implementation documentation and source repositories; then ERC-4626 security research, audits and incident analyses; then the broader academic literature on lending, liquidations, oracles, leverage and collateralized stablecoins; finally current 2025–2026 work on curation, vault credit and asynchronous/NAV standards. Current EIP statuses and current product documentation were checked through **September 8, 2026**. citeturn17view0turn18view0turn18view3turn18view4turn23search0turn14search2

The screening rule favored normative specifications, official protocol documentation, canonical repositories, peer-reviewed papers, arXiv originals, security-firm technical analyses, audits and protocol postmortems. Generic price/TVL pages, duplicate mirrors, marketing announcements without technical content and unsupported incident summaries were not used as primary evidence. Recent papers such as the 2025 curator study, 2026 vault-credit paper and 2026 liquidation-fee paper are explicitly labeled **preprints** rather than promoted to established results. citeturn19academia11turn19academia12turn19academia13

The review does **not** claim exhaustive systematic-review coverage. Some audit and formal-verification PDFs were discoverable through official protocol indexes—Liquity's current technical-security page, for example, lists multiple independent audits and a Certora formal-verification report—but detailed findings from a PDF were not promoted into this report unless an inspectable textual source independently supported the claim. citeturn14search7

                         ↓

                         ↓

**ERC-4626 standardizes the top of this diagram extremely usefully. The research problem of collateral vaults is everything required to make the arrows below it trustworthy.** The current literature demonstrates that those arrows can fail through rounding, direct donations, stale or manipulable valuations, recursive leverage, asynchronous settlement, privileged allocation, inadequate liquidity and liquidation feedback. Standards such as ERC-7540, ERC-7575 and emerging NAV interfaces increasingly expose the relevant state, while modern projects such as Euler, Morpho, Centrifuge, Silo, Yearn and Aave explore different architectural decompositions. The remaining opportunity is to turn those pieces into **compositional, assumption-explicit safety properties** that connect vault accounting to recoverable collateral value. citeturn17view0turn18view0turn18view4turn23view0turn19academia11turn19academia13
