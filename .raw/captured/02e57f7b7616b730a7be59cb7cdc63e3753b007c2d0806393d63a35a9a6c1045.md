# Modern DeFi Taxonomy and Ethereum Standards Atlas

**Research cutoff and verification date:** September 8, 2026, America/Denver.  
**Scope:** financial taxonomy is chain-agnostic; the standards atlas is Ethereum/EVM-focused. “Current” below means verified against sources available through the cutoff. Formal standards status, deployed implementation, economic adoption, and security evidence are treated as separate facts rather than proxies for one another.

The core conclusion is that modern DeFi is best represented **not as one mutually exclusive tree of protocol brands**, but as a small hierarchy of **financial functions** connected to independent dimensions describing mechanisms, claims, lifecycle, standards, execution, authority, dependencies, and risks.

The recommended data model is:

> **financial function → mechanism → asset/position → interface relationship → deployed component → dependency/trust graph → risk**

A protocol brand can occupy several nodes; a particular market, vault, token, or deployment is the preferred unit of classification.

## Executive taxonomy map

The four supplied papers remain valuable, but they classify different things and therefore should not be fused literally.

Gogol et al. select economically important categories principally using December 18, 2022 TVL and then propose three algorithmic classes—**liquidity pool, synthetic asset, and aggregator**—alongside separate token and network taxonomies. Their Figure 1 on page 2 also presents trading, lending, asset management, and interoperability as a value-proposition hierarchy. fileciteturn0file0

Kotzer et al. are much more narrowly focused on lending. They distinguish lending primitives from the protocols that instantiate them and from yield aggregators that compose those protocols. Their Table II separates overcollateralized, undercollateralized, zero-liquidation, and flash loans along dimensions including duration, funding, interest rates, and leverage. Their cross-protocol comparison is explicitly a January 2025 snapshot, while the empirical Aave/Compound work is historical rather than a 2026 market snapshot. fileciteturn0file1

Zhou et al. classify the **system and threat surface**, rather than attempting a pure financial-product taxonomy. Their five layers—network, consensus, smart contract, protocol, and auxiliary services—remain exceptionally useful as a dependency and risk overlay. Particularly important for a standards atlas, their protocol-level attack taxonomy recognizes both token-standard incompatibility and malicious contracts that mimic an expected ABI while behaving differently. fileciteturn0file2

Werner et al. classify DeFi by primitives and six operational types—asset exchange, loanable-funds markets, stablecoins, portfolio management, derivatives, and privacy-preserving mixers—and then distinguish technical from economic security **by atomicity**, not simply by “code risk” versus “market risk.” Their technical-security definition concerns attacks that can be executed atomically or effectively risk-free; their economic-security category concerns strictly non-atomic exploitation where time and market reaction matter. fileciteturn0file3

### Recommended canonical hierarchy

The most durable replacement is an eight-root hierarchy:

| Stable ID | Financial function | Principal subfunctions | Classification question |
|---|---|---|---|
| **FF.MNY** | **Money, payments, and settlement** | monetary issuance/redemption; payments, escrow and settlement | Is the component creating, redeeming, transferring, or settling a money-like obligation? |
| **FF.EXE** | **Exchange, price discovery, and trade execution** | venues/price discovery; routing; aggregation; intent/solver execution | Is its principal service exchanging already-existing claims or arranging execution? |
| **FF.CRD** | **Credit, lending, and collateral management** | pooled/isolated lending; collateralized debt issuance; term/private credit | Does someone obtain present liquidity against a repayment, collateral, or default obligation? |
| **FF.CAP** | **Capital formation and treasury services** | primary issuance/fundraising; treasury deployment | Is new organizational/project capital being raised, issued, or allocated? |
| **FF.DER** | **Derivatives and contingent claims** | futures/perpetuals; options/structured payoffs; principal-yield/rate claims; event claims | Does payoff depend on a price, rate, event, funding stream, maturity, or future state? |
| **FF.AMG** | **Asset management and vault systems** | managed vaults; allocators/curators; indexes/portfolios; automated liquidity management | Has continuing portfolio allocation or strategy execution been delegated? |
| **FF.SEC** | **Staking and security services** | staking/delegation; liquid staking; restaking/shared security | Is capital being compensated for consensus or security-service participation? |
| **FF.RSK** | **Risk transfer and loss allocation** | cover; guarantees/backstops; senior/junior or first-loss structures | Is one party explicitly paid or capitalized to absorb specified losses of another? |

These roots deliberately exclude several familiar “DeFi categories.” **Stablecoin, vault, RWA, bridge, aggregator, AMM, solver, yield farming, app-chain, and cross-chain** are not all the same kind of object.

The crucial reclassification is:

- A **stablecoin** is primarily an asset/claim property; its issuer can simultaneously perform `FF.MNY` and `FF.CRD`.
- A **vault** is a container/accounting and lifecycle pattern; it becomes `FF.AMG` only when it actually delegates investment or allocation.
- **RWA** identifies the provenance and legal/trust structure of an underlying claim, not a financial function.
- A **bridge/cross-chain system** identifies an execution, messaging, verification, or settlement topology.
- An **aggregator** describes routing or composition.
- An **AMM, CLOB, auction, RFQ, utilization curve, liquidation auction, or solver network** is a mechanism.
- **Yield farming** is primarily a user strategy, although a product may automate that strategy as asset management.
- **ERC-4626** is an accounting/interface standard, not a synonym for asset management.

That separation is increasingly necessary because current systems compose these ideas independently. Uniswap v4, for example, separates a core PoolManager from configurable hooks and custom accounting, meaning a “hook” is an extensibility mechanism rather than a financial-product category. citeturn19search0turn19search1turn19search3 CoW Protocol uses combinatorial batch auctions and solver-based execution, while 1inch Fusion uses intent-like orders and resolver competition; neither is naturally described merely as a “liquidity pool.” citeturn18search0turn19search6turn19search7

Likewise, current ERC design has become explicitly multi-dimensional. ERC-4626 standardizes tokenized-vault accounting, ERC-7540 adds asynchronous request lifecycles, and ERC-7575 accommodates multiple asset entry points and externalized share tokens. Those are separable properties, not mutually exclusive financial products. citeturn12view0turn12view1turn12view2

### The independent classification dimensions

Every classified object should carry the following facets in addition to its financial-function label.

| Dimension | Recommended fields |
|---|---|
| **Mechanism** | AMM/CFMM, concentrated liquidity, order book, batch auction, RFQ, solver competition, pooled lending, isolated market, peer matching, CDP, utilization-rate curve, fixed term, liquidation auction, tranche, oracle settlement, etc. |
| **Asset and claim** | direct asset, reserve claim, pool share, vault share, debt claim, staking receipt, derivative payoff, fund/security claim, wrapped asset, legal/off-chain claim |
| **Funding and return source** | borrower interest; trading fees; consensus rewards; security-service payments; derivative funding/basis; external-asset income; insurance premium; token subsidy |
| **Lifecycle and liquidity** | transaction-atomic, synchronous, queued, epoch-based, request/claim, fixed maturity, open-ended, conditionally redeemable |
| **Interface and standards** | implements, accepts, adapts to, wraps, or merely co-uses an ERC/interface |
| **Execution/deployment** | single-chain; rollup; app-chain; multichain deployment; off-chain matching/on-chain settlement; cross-chain execution |
| **Authority and trust** | permissionless admission; allowlist; curator; oracle committee; admin/upgrade key; pause/freeze/forced-transfer authority; legal/custodial dependency |
| **Dependencies and risk** | tokens, protocols, vaults, oracles, bridges, relayers/solvers, custodians, issuers, legal vehicles, sequencers, governance |

This architecture also solves the TVL problem in P1. TVL was a defensible sampling mechanism for a 2022 survey, but it is not a good ontology rule. fileciteturn0file0 A modern exchange should be assessed with trading volume and liquidity as well as locked assets; credit with outstanding debt and available liquidity; monetary systems with issued supply and redemption activity; derivatives with open interest and volume; asset management with managed assets; and risk-transfer products with active cover/risk capital. Recursive positions also make additive TVL misleading: an underlying asset may support a staking claim, which is then deposited as lending collateral, which may itself be represented by another receipt. The same economic capital can therefore appear at multiple points in a composability graph.

## Canonical categories and classification rules

The primary rule is **classify the smallest economically meaningful object first**. “Aave,” “Uniswap,” “Morpho,” or “Lido” is too coarse when a brand exposes several components. A reserve, market, pool, vault, token, position, strategy, and deployment can receive different labels.

### Category reference

| Function | Include | Exclude or place elsewhere | Typical claims and mechanisms | Important neighbors |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **FF.MNY** | issuance/redemption of money-like claims; payment settlement; escrow | secondary exchange → EXE; borrowing of an existing asset → CRD | redeemable tokens, reserve-backed claims, debt-issued stable assets | CRD where money is issued by borrowing; CAP where issuer creates security-like claims |
| **FF.EXE** | spot exchange, order matching, auctions, routing, RFQ, aggregators, intent execution | derivative payoff creation → DER; continuing portfolio delegation → AMG | AMM LP positions, signed orders, batch auctions, solver orders | DER when the traded object is a derivative; MNY for cross-chain payment settlement |
| **FF.CRD** | loans, credit lines, isolated markets, CDPs, private credit, term credit | atomic capital as a standalone ontology root; investment strategy → AMG | supply receipts, debt balances, collateral positions, liquidation rules | MNY for minted stable debt; AMG for lending allocators |
| **FF.CAP** | primary token/security/bond issuance; fundraising; treasury deployment | secondary token trading; ordinary DAO voting | primary claims, bonds, allocation mandates | CRD for debt capital; AMG for investment-fund management |
| **FF.DER** | futures, perpetuals, options, rate/yield claims, event contracts, payoff-replicating synthetics | redeemable receipt whose main economics are ownership of underlying | margined positions, PT/YT, contingent tokens, funding mechanisms | EXE for the venue; SEC if an LST represents staked principal rather than synthetic payoff |
| **FF.AMG** | vault strategy, curated allocation, rebalancing, index, fund | pure custody vault; borrower collateral vault; accounting interface alone | ERC-4626 shares, managed fund shares, allocator strategies | CRD if capital allocated to loans; EXE if strategy manages LP positions |
| **FF.SEC** | staking, liquid staking, shared-security services | ordinary lending/yield farming | staking receipts, rebasing or share-based LSTs, slashing exposure | AMG where staking assets are managed; DER only where payoff is synthesized rather than a redeemable staking claim |
| **FF.RSK** | cover, mutual risk pool, guarantee, first-loss, tranche | ordinary collateral and liquidation rules unless explicitly sold as risk transfer | cover contracts, risk-pool capital, tranches | CRD for credit-loss protection; DER for fully formulaic contingent payoffs |

The **stablecoin boundary** illustrates why multi-function classification matters. Liquity V2 allows users to create BOLD through collateralized Troves and charges user-selected borrowing interest; therefore the issuance component is simultaneously a money-issuance function and a collateralized-credit function. citeturn22search0turn22search4 Ethena’s USDe has a fundamentally different backing and hedging architecture, involving crypto assets and derivative positions, while direct mint/redemption access is permissioned to approved counterparties even though the token circulates permissionlessly. Its staked form, sUSDe, distributes returns associated with the system’s revenue sources. citeturn22search11turn22search13 Grouping both only as “algorithmic stablecoins” would discard the most important distinctions.

The **liquid-staking boundary** similarly shows why “synthetic asset” is too broad. Lido’s stETH is a rebasing token tied to staked ETH and staking rewards, while wstETH is a non-rebasing share-style wrapper whose exchange relationship to stETH changes as rewards accrue. These are economically claims on a staking system, not merely tokens designed to mimic an external spot price. citeturn23search1turn23search6turn23search12 P1’s decision to place liquid-staking tokens, wrapped bridge tokens, and crypto-backed stablecoins together as “synthetic assets” is historically understandable, but modern claim analysis should split them by **backing, redemption, cash flow, and obligations**. fileciteturn0file0

### Function × mechanism × claim matrix

| Financial function | Characteristic mechanisms | Characteristic claim | Typical return source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Money | reserve issuance; collateralized minting; redemption queue; transfer/escrow | money-like token or redeemable claim | normally none to holder; optional reserve/yield pass-through |
| Exchange | CFMM/AMM; concentrated liquidity; order book; batch auction; RFQ; solver | traded asset; LP claim; signed order | trading fees, spread, solver surplus |
| Credit | pooled/isolated market; utilization curve; fixed term; CDP; liquidation | lender receipt, borrower debt, collateral position | borrower interest and fees |
| Capital formation | auction; primary issuance; subscription; bond issuance | security, token, debt, project claim | issuer/project cash flows or appreciation |
| Derivatives | margin engine; oracle mark; funding; option exercise; PT/YT split | contingent/maturity payoff | counterparty loss, funding, underlying yield |
| Asset management | vault accounting; allocator; curator; rebalancing | fund/vault/portfolio share | underlying strategies less fees |
| Staking/security | validator delegation; pooled staking; reusable security collateral | staking receipt or security-service claim | consensus/security-service rewards |
| Risk transfer | premium pool; discretionary claims; parametric payout; tranche | cover/right to payout/risk capital | premiums and risk compensation |

The **source of yield is never “the vault.”** A vault may only redistribute underlying economics. For example, Morpho Vault V2 exposes an ERC-4626-compatible share layer while allocations can route through approved adapters into underlying markets; the curator/allocator function and the underlying borrower interest are distinct objects. citeturn21search10turn21search1turn21search2 Pendle, by contrast, wraps yield-bearing assets and separates principal and yield rights into PT and YT positions; the wrapper itself does not manufacture the underlying yield. citeturn22search5turn22search7turn22search8

A useful attribution rule is:

> **Return = underlying operating cash flow or transfer payment + explicit subsidy − fees − realized losses.**

Thus borrower interest belongs to credit; trading fees to exchange/liquidity provision; staking rewards to security service; derivative funding/basis to derivatives; external bond/fund income to the external asset; premiums to risk transfer; and governance-token emissions to **subsidy**, not organic financial yield.

### Decision rules for ambiguous systems

| Question | Classification rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Is “vault” enough to classify a product? | **No.** Determine the obligation first. | Liquity’s “Trove” is a borrower debt/collateral position, whereas an ERC-4626 allocator is pooled asset management. citeturn22search0turn12view0 |
| Is a flash loan its own peer-level financial sector? | Usually **no**. Record atomic, uncollateralized liquidity as a lifecycle/execution capability of a credit or liquidity component. | ERC-3156 standardizes a flash-loan callback/interface, not an entire lending-market ontology. citeturn11view3 |
| Is an LST a derivative? | Not automatically. Examine redemption/backing. | stETH/wstETH are better classified as staking claims; a token with only replicated price exposure would be DER. citeturn23search1turn23search6 |
| Is “RWA” a category? | **No: use it as an underlying/legal-trust facet.** | OUSG is economically a tokenized investment/fund claim with eligibility and redemption rules; “RWA” describes its off-chain asset/legal dependency. citeturn23search0turn23search2turn23search3 |
| Is cross-chain a category? | Usually **no: use an execution/settlement facet.** | Across can execute a cross-chain transfer, swap, or destination action; those user outcomes remain payments or exchange. citeturn24search4turn24search8 |
| Is an aggregator asset management? | Only when it has continuing allocation discretion. | A DEX router is EXE.RTE; a yield allocator holding user capital over time is AMG.ALL. |
| Is ERC compliance economic equivalence? | **Never infer that.** | Two ERC-4626 vaults can expose the same API yet contain entirely different assets, authority, liquidity, and loss structures. citeturn12view0 |

The architecture also generalizes outside EVM. The intent/solver concept can be used on non-EVM execution environments; for example, 1inch has documented Fusion-style execution on Solana. The financial classifier remains `FF.EXE`; the Ethereum ERC facets are simply absent or replaced by the relevant native interfaces. citeturn19search10

## Ethereum standards atlas and crosswalk

EIP-1 is the first guardrail: **ERC is one Standards Track category**, alongside Core, Networking, and Interface. Therefore EIP-712 is an Interface EIP rather than an ERC, while account/delegation changes at the consensus or transaction layer such as EIP-7702 must not be relabeled “ERCs” merely because DeFi software uses them. EIP-1 also distinguishes Draft, Review, Last Call, Final, Stagnant, Withdrawn, and Living statuses, and defines `requires` as a normative dependency relationship. citeturn3view0

### Vault semantics are now a family, not “4626-compatible = interchangeable”

ERC-4626’s basic accounting model relates an ERC-20 asset to fungible vault shares. `asset()` and `totalAssets()` identify the accounting base; `convertToShares`/`convertToAssets` provide idealized conversion estimates; `preview*` functions model an immediate operation more closely; `max*` functions constrain what a caller can actually do; and `deposit`, `mint`, `withdraw`, and `redeem` perform the state transitions. The specification itself separates ideal conversion from fees/slippage and warns about integration/security assumptions. citeturn12view0

ERC-7540 changes the lifecycle: the user can first submit a request, which can move through **pending → claimable → claimed** states. For an asynchronous path, the ordinary synchronous 4626 preview semantics are not simply preserved; the standard deliberately modifies behavior because the eventual exchange may not be known or executable at request time. This is particularly relevant to tokenized external assets, undercollateralized credit, cross-chain operations, staking withdrawals, and insurance-like modules. citeturn12view1

ERC-7575 changes the topology: multiple asset-specific vault entry points can share an external share token. Consequently an integration that assumes “the 4626 vault contract is itself the ERC-20 share token” can be wrong even when the system conforms to the newer architecture. citeturn12view2 ERC-7535 makes a separate behavioral adaptation for native assets. citeturn12view3

Therefore:

> **“ERC-4626-like accounting,” “ERC-7540 asynchronous lifecycle,” and “ERC-7575 multi-asset topology” must be separate graph properties.**

### ERC-7683 is especially important to version correctly

The current ERC-7683 design should not be described using its older public draft. The current proposal moves the interoperability boundary toward a **solver-facing resolver model**: protocol-specific order data can be resolved off-chain through `eth_call` into a common description of steps, variables, and payments. The specification explicitly explains that an earlier draft attempted a broader standardized lifecycle using structures such as `OnchainCrossChainOrder`, `GaslessCrossChainOrder`, origin settlers, destination `fill`, and `ResolvedCrossChainOrder`. That approach was rejected because it embedded shallow implementation-specific fields and assumptions—particularly escrow-before-fill—that did not generalize to resource locks or fill-first designs. citeturn14view0

This is a strong example of why a standards atlas needs **revision-sensitive semantics**, not merely “ERC-7683 = cross-chain intents.”

### Standards that belong in the long tail

Several standards are useful but should not be treated as universal foundations.

ERC-3009 remains useful as a signed-transfer authorization design distinct from ERC-2612’s allowance-oriented permit model, while ERC-4494 applies permit-style approval to ERC-721 but is Stagnant. citeturn9view2turn11view1 ERC-777 and ERC-1820 remain historically important for token hooks and interface registration, and ERC-1363 offers payable callback-style ERC-20 interactions, but callbacks also illustrate why syntactic compatibility can introduce reentrancy or unexpected control transfer into protocols designed around simpler token assumptions. citeturn5view1turn9view1turn9view0

For yield claims, ERC-5095’s principal-token proposal is Stagnant, while ERC-5115’s SY interface is a Draft designed around more flexible wrapping of yield-bearing assets than a conventional single-asset vault. Pendle’s actual SY/PT/YT design is therefore best treated as a deployed protocol convention plus relevant proposed standards rather than evidence that the ecosystem has one universal yield-token interface. citeturn7view0turn11view2turn22search5

ERC-7092 proposes an explicitly financial-bond interface with coupon, maturity and principal concepts and is deliberately not backward-compatible with ERC-20/1155 in its pure form; an identity registry can be layered on top, underscoring again that token representation and regulatory identity are separate dimensions. citeturn25search0

The 2025–2026 vault research frontier includes proposals for **transferable asynchronous requests** in ERC-8161, **cancellation of ERC-7540 requests** in ERC-7887, and series-style accounting/performance-fee handling in ERC-8113. These should be tracked as evolving extensions, not prematurely incorporated into a stable core taxonomy. citeturn2search0turn2search4turn2search2 Proposed basket-token and solvency-proof interfaces similarly illustrate active standardization without proving broad adoption. citeturn0search7turn2search3

| **FF.RSK** | base token interfaces; asynchronous vault patterns may be usable for capital pools | claim assessment, coverage wording, payout adjudication, tranching and solvency logic remain mostly bespoke |

The reverse mapping reinforces why ERCs are **capability standards** rather than financial categories. ERC-20 appears across almost everything; ERC-4626 can appear in asset management, lending, staking wrappers, and external-asset funds; ERC-7540 describes a lifecycle used by several different economic products; ERC-1271 concerns authorization irrespective of the financial function. citeturn4view0turn12view0turn12view1turn6view1

The official ERC set reviewed through the cutoff does **not** support a credible claim that there is one universal ERC for lending markets, AMMs, price oracles, perpetuals, or insurance. In those areas, protocol-specific interfaces still dominate. ERC-3668, for example, standardizes how a client can retrieve and return off-chain data for contract verification; it does **not** standardize a financial price feed or guarantee the economic truth of the data. citeturn24search0turn24search15

## Dependency, compatibility, and paper crosswalk

The standards graph needs typed relationships rather than a generic “inherits from” arrow.

Recommended edge types are:

`IMPLEMENTS`, `EXTENDS`, `REQUIRES`, `ACCEPTS_ASSET`, `ADAPTS_TO`, `WRAPS`, `ISSUES`, `BACKED_BY`, `REDEEMS_FOR`, `YIELD_FROM`, `DEPENDS_ON`, `GOVERNED_BY`, `AUTHORIZED_BY`, `PRICED_BY`, `SETTLES_ON`, and `MESSAGES_VIA`.

This distinction matters technically. A lending market that accepts an ERC-20 does not “implement ERC-20.” A vault adapter that can interact with ERC-4626 shares does not necessarily implement ERC-4626. A protocol can resemble a specification while deliberately deviating from its required semantics.

Zhou et al. provide the security reason: interface conformity is not behavioral conformity. Their system model explicitly allows an attacker to deploy a customized contract that mimics another protocol’s function interface, while their incident taxonomy contains token-standard incompatibility as an unsafe dependency. fileciteturn0file2

### Compatibility map

| Relationship | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Normative dependency** | Standard A requires concepts/interfaces from B | ERC-2612 depends on ERC-20 and EIP-712. citeturn6view0 |
| **Extension** | Adds behavior to another standard | ERC-7540 extends 4626 with async requests. citeturn12view1 |
| **Alternative topology** | Similar purpose, different contract layout/semantics | ERC-7575 externalizes the share token and permits multiple entry points. citeturn12view2 |
| **Accepted asset** | Protocol consumes a token implementing an interface | A lending market may accept ERC-20 collateral without itself being ERC-20. |
| **Adapter compatibility** | Separate contract translates interfaces | Morpho Vault V2 uses approved adapters to route to underlying opportunities. citeturn21search2 |
| **Common co-use** | Standards commonly appear together but neither requires the other | 4626 vault interactions may be signed through account/authorization standards. |
| **Behavioral incompatibility** | ABI/types appear acceptable but economics or callbacks violate assumptions | Hook/callback tokens, fee-on-transfer assets, rebasing assets, or adversarial look-alikes can break integrations. fileciteturn0file2 |

The strongest conceptual break with P1 is therefore **not that its observations were wrong in 2022**. It is that “liquidity pool,” “aggregator,” and “synthetic asset” classify different ontological levels. A pool is generally a funding/container mechanism; an aggregator is a coordination/composition role; and a synthetic is a property of a claim or payoff. Modern composability makes those properties many-to-many rather than mutually exclusive.

### Authority and decentralization should also be a vector

Permissionless/permissioned should not be inferred from the token interface. Maple documents permissioned pools with borrower/lender eligibility controls while using smart-contract pool structures and ERC-4626-style share accounting. citeturn21search0 Ondo’s OUSG similarly couples on-chain tokenization with eligibility/onboarding and external fund/assets. citeturn23search0turn23search2 ERC-3643 explicitly provides identity/compliance registry architecture, while ERC-7943 standardizes a thinner layer of transfer restriction, freezing and forced transfer. citeturn17view1turn17view0

Accordingly, “DeFi versus CeFi” is better recorded with separate fields for:

**custody; user admission; transfer censorship; parameter control; upgradeability; oracle control; asset custody; redemption authority; legal dependence; and settlement venue.**

That generalizes P1’s useful observation that an on-chain token can still retain centralized issuer controls. fileciteturn0file0

### Risk overlay

The best synthesis of P3 and P4 is to keep security orthogonal to financial function.

| Risk axis | What to record | Typical examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Implementation** | code correctness, access control, call assumptions | reentrancy, rounding, faulty authorization, callback behavior |
| **Interface/behavioral** | whether accepted contracts behave as integrator assumes | fee-on-transfer/rebasing assets, hook tokens, ABI camouflage, non-standard return behavior. fileciteturn0file2 |
| **Valuation/oracle** | source, update cadence, manipulation window, fallback | stale prices, manipulable spot oracles, external market divergence. fileciteturn0file3 |
| **Collateral/credit** | collateral quality, LLTV, liquidation, bad-debt allocation | price gaps, unavailable liquidators, borrower default |
| **Liquidity/lifecycle** | whether quoted/accounting value can actually be exited | high utilization, withdrawal queues, maturity, async claims; ERC-4626 conversion is not a liquidity guarantee. citeturn12view0turn12view1 |
| **Authority** | upgrade, freeze, pause, curator, oracle and forced-transfer powers | permissioned credit, RWA transfer controls, emergency governance |
| **Dependency/composition** | upstream protocols/assets and recursive positions | nested vaults, LST collateral, leveraged loops, adapters |
| **Execution** | ordering, solver/relayer and finality assumptions | MEV, batch/solver behavior, cross-chain fill/settlement |
| **Legal/custodial** | issuer, custodian, SPV/fund and enforceable redemption rights | tokenized securities/external funds |
| **Atomicity** | whether exploitation is risk-free/atomic or unfolds over time | preserve Werner et al.’s technical-versus-economic distinction. fileciteturn0file3 |

Flash loans deserve special treatment here. ERC-3156 makes atomic liquidity a standardized capability. citeturn11view3 A flash loan can supply capital used in an exploit, but its existence does not identify the root vulnerability. P3’s model and P4’s atomicity analysis both support tracing the actual unsafe dependency, oracle, authorization, or protocol invariant rather than writing “flash-loan attack” as though capital access itself were the bug. fileciteturn0file2 fileciteturn0file3

## Vocabulary and research atlas

The following are the **working definitions for this taxonomy**, intended to remove overloaded industry usage.

| Term | Definition in this reference |
|---|---|
| **Protocol family** | Related set of protocol designs/products under one project or lineage. |
| **Protocol version** | Specific major implementation/design generation, such as V2/V3/V4. |
| **Deployment** | Instance of code/configuration on one chain/environment. |
| **Component** | Functionally distinct smart-contract subsystem. |
| **Market** | Parameterized venue where a specific asset pair, credit pair, derivative, or other obligation is formed. |
| **Vault instance** | Particular vault contract/accounting pool, not the abstract concept “vault.” |
| **Financial function** | Economic service or obligation performed for users; primary taxonomy axis. |
| **Mechanism** | Rule by which a function determines prices, rates, allocation, liquidation, settlement or payout. |
| **Facet** | Independent descriptive dimension that can coexist with multiple categories. |
| **Implementation pattern** | Reusable technical architecture such as singleton, hook, adapter, or solver. |
| **Narrative** | Market label such as “RWA,” “intent-centric,” or “DeFi 2.0” that should not substitute for a definition. |
| **Asset** | Transferable or recorded item of value. |
| **Claim** | Economic, contractual, legal, or protocol right represented by a position or token. |
| **Position** | User-specific state carrying rights and/or obligations. |
| **Receipt token** | Token evidencing a deposited, staked, lent, or otherwise encumbered position. |
| **Share** | Proportional claim on a pool/vault accounting base; in ERC-4626, shares are the vault-side units. citeturn12view0 |
| **Wrapped claim** | Token obtained by wrapping another asset/claim, normally preserving some redeemability relation. |
| **Synthetic exposure** | Payoff designed to replicate or depend on another variable without necessarily representing redeemable ownership of it. |
| **Collateral** | Asset/claim pledged to secure an obligation. |
| **Debt** | Obligation to repay or settle value. |
| **Collateralized debt position** | Debt position whose enforceability/loss protection relies materially on pledged collateral. |
| **Lending pool** | Capital pool from which borrowers draw loans. |
| **Isolated market** | Credit/trading market whose risk parameters and balance sheet are intentionally separated from other markets. |
| **Utilization** | Fraction of supplied/available lending capital currently borrowed, where applicable. |
| **Liquidation** | Process of reducing/closing an unsafe collateralized position by converting/seizing collateral. |
| **Bad debt** | Debt not fully covered/recovered under the protocol’s loss process. |
| **Flash liquidity** | Capital borrowed/used under atomic repayment constraints rather than long-duration credit risk. |
| **Vault** | Generic overloaded word; may denote custody, borrower collateral, pooled investment accounting, or strategy. Never classify from the word alone. |
| **Tokenized vault** | Vault exposing standardized asset/share accounting such as ERC-4626; not automatically an investment fund. citeturn12view0 |
| **Allocator** | Actor/contract deciding how pooled capital is distributed among underlying opportunities. |
| **Curator** | Authority defining or supervising eligible markets/assets/parameters for an allocation product. |
| **Strategy** | Sequence/rule for deploying capital; conceptually distinct from the vault holding the capital. |
| **Aggregator** | Component combining routes, liquidity sources, prices, or underlying strategies; classification depends on what it aggregates. |
| **AMM** | Mechanism using algorithmic pricing/liquidity rules rather than ordinary bilateral order matching. |
| **Concentrated liquidity** | Liquidity provision restricted to selected price intervals. |
| **Batch auction** | Mechanism matching/settling a set of orders collectively rather than strictly sequentially. |
| **RFQ** | Request-for-quote execution where makers/solvers quote terms for a requested trade. |
| **Intent** | User expression of a desired outcome while delegating some execution-path choice. |
| **Solver/resolver** | Actor that finds or supplies execution satisfying an intent/order subject to protocol rules. |
| **Yield** | Economic return over time; must always be traced to an underlying payer, activity, risk premium, or subsidy. |
| **Principal token** | Maturity-oriented claim primarily representing principal redemption rights. |
| **Yield token** | Claim on some or all yield generated by an underlying position for a defined period. |
| **Liquid staking token** | Transferable claim tied to staked capital and associated staking economics. |
| **Restaking** | Reuse/extension of staked economic security to additional security services; treat separately from lending and leverage. |
| **Stablecoin** | Token targeting relatively stable value against a reference; the term alone does not specify backing or issuer function. |
| **RWA** | Underlying/provenance/legal-trust facet indicating dependence on externally originated assets or legal rights. |
| **Redemption** | Exchange of a claim for its defined underlying or settlement asset under specified conditions. |
| **Accounting value** | Protocol-calculated value/share conversion; may differ from realizable exit proceeds. |
| **Exit liquidity** | Assets actually available for withdrawal/redemption at the relevant time. |
| **Synchronous lifecycle** | Request and settlement can occur in one execution flow under current state. |
| **Asynchronous lifecycle** | Request precedes later claimability/settlement, as formalized in ERC-7540. citeturn12view1 |
| **Atomicity** | All-or-nothing execution boundary; transaction-, bundle-, and cross-chain contexts must be distinguished. fileciteturn0file3 |
| **Composability** | Ability to combine protocol state transitions/interfaces; does not imply combined safety. fileciteturn0file2 |
| **Interface compatibility** | Syntactic ability to call expected functions. |
| **Behavioral compatibility** | Implementation obeys behavioral assumptions needed by the caller. |
| **Economic compatibility** | Claim/liquidity/risk semantics are suitable for the consuming protocol; stronger than ABI compatibility. |

### Maintenance rules

A new system should be added without changing the hierarchy unless it introduces a genuinely new **financial obligation**, not merely a new implementation method.

The maintenance procedure is:

| Test | Action |
|---|---|
| New protocol uses an existing economic function with a new mechanism | Add mechanism value; do **not** add top-level category. |
| New token represents a novel claim | Add claim subtype and redemption/backing fields. |
| New ERC standardizes an interface capability | Add standard node and typed relationship; do not create financial category. |
| New cross-chain implementation | Add execution/messaging/verification facets. |
| New “RWA” product | Classify its true function, then add external-asset/legal/custody dependencies. |
| New aggregator | Ask whether it routes transactions or manages capital over time. |
| New vault | Determine economic obligation before adding AMG label. |
| New staking/restaking product | Record security service, withdrawal/slashing, operator and collateral-reuse obligations. |
| New risk product | Distinguish contractual/parametric payout from discretionary claim assessment. |

For standards, retain at least five independent evidence fields: **formal specification status, implementation maturity, deployment evidence, economic adoption, and security evidence**. Final status can prove that the standard completed its process; it cannot prove deployment, economic importance, safety, decentralization, legal validity, or suitability for a particular claim. EIP-1’s status model and the economic diversity of 4626 implementations make that separation necessary. citeturn3view0turn12view0

### Unresolved boundaries and standardization gaps

**Vault lifecycle remains an active standards frontier.** ERC-4626, 7540 and 7575 now cover much more of the design space than existed when the older SoKs were written, but transferable pending requests, cancellation semantics, complex fee accounting and series-based accounting are still being explored in newer proposals such as ERC-8161, ERC-7887 and ERC-8113. citeturn2search0turn2search4turn2search2

**Lending remains economically standardized far less than vault accounting.** Current major protocols expose materially different market/account models—Aave’s pooled reserves, Compound III’s base-asset structure, Morpho’s isolated markets, and Morpho Midnight’s term structure—without one broadly established ERC that captures collateral eligibility, interest model, liquidation, debt balances and market discovery across all of them. citeturn20search10turn21search4turn20search0turn20search9

**Derivatives remain predominantly bespoke.** Principal/yield token proposals exist, but perpetual margin, funding, liquidation and position interfaces are still largely protocol-specific; GMX is a current example of an oracle-priced perpetual architecture whose economic semantics are not captured by the generic token standards it uses. citeturn7view0turn24search2

**Oracle/data standardization must not be confused with truth standardization.** ERC-3668 can standardize retrieval and verification routing while leaving the validation method implementation-specific; it cannot tell a lending or derivative protocol what an economically correct ETH/USD price is. citeturn24search0

**Cross-chain interoperability is improving at the interface layer but not converging on one security model.** ERC-7683’s current design standardizes the solver-facing resolution boundary rather than one settlement implementation, ERC-7786 standardizes generic messaging, and ERC-7802 standardizes a cross-chain mint/burn interface. None makes heterogeneous bridges, solvers or chain-finality assumptions economically equivalent. citeturn14view0turn14view1turn14view2

**RWA standards cannot encode the entire claim.** ERC-3643 and ERC-7943 can standardize identity/control or transfer-restriction mechanics, while systems such as OUSG still depend on issuers, legal documents, eligible-investor rules, custodial arrangements and external assets. A token passing `canTransfer` is therefore not evidence that the holder has a particular legally enforceable redemption claim. citeturn17view1turn17view0turn23search0

**Restaking/shared-security semantics remain better treated as a financial-function family plus security-obligation facets than as “yield farming.”** The essential classifier is who receives security, what capital can be slashed, what service earns the reward, what withdrawal conditions apply, and whether the same collateral secures multiple obligations. A future standard should expose those semantics rather than merely standardizing the transferability of a receipt token.

**Behavioral conformance remains the central composability gap.** ERC interfaces make components easier to connect, but P3’s unsafe-dependency results remain directly relevant: an accepted interface can conceal callbacks, unusual accounting, incompatible transfer behavior, or adversarial semantics. fileciteturn0file2 ERC-4626’s security considerations and current Morpho material on donation, rounding, share-value manipulation, and collateralized vault shares show the same problem in a modern vault context. citeturn12view0turn21search6

The resulting canonical model can therefore be summarized in one rule:

> **Classify DeFi first by the economic obligation it creates; describe how that obligation works with mechanism and claim facets; describe how software can interact with it using standards; and describe whether it can be trusted by explicitly modeling lifecycle, authority, dependencies, and exit.**

That model preserves the strongest insights of all four SoKs while avoiding their principal age-related limitation: modern DeFi is no longer adequately described by making protocols, algorithms, tokens, networks, strategies, wrappers, and infrastructure compete for the same branch of one taxonomy. fileciteturn0file0 fileciteturn0file1 fileciteturn0file2 fileciteturn0file3
