# Translation validation versus a verified compiler for Core to ZKIR

**Recommendation.** Do per-program validation, but not in the black-box form the proposal describes. Build a *credible compiler*: the Core-to-ZKIR compiler emits, with each contract, the simulation relation between Core states and ZKIR witness assignments (which Core variable is which field element, which obligation is which constraint block), and a small checker discharges the resulting equivalence obligations against the two K semantics. Verify the checker once. Do not attempt to infer the relation from the two programs alone: every black-box validator in the literature that bridges a large gap tops out at 90-97% of functions with hours-long timeouts, and none of them faced a target whose meaning is "there exists a witness satisfying these constraints" rather than a deterministic program. Full compiler verification (CompCert, CakeML) is the wrong bet at this stage: its cost is measured in tens of thousands of proof lines and person-years, it is paid again whenever the compiler changes, and the two semantics here are still moving. The verified-validator route gives the same soundness guarantee for roughly a tenth of the proof effort, at the price of possible spurious rejections.

## 1. The founding work

**Pnueli, Siegel, Singerman, "Translation Validation", TACAS 1998** (https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/BFb0054170; abstract and references retrieved, full PDF not). They validated the SACRES code generator from the synchronous data-flow language Signal to sequential C. The three ingredients they name are exactly our problem: a common semantic framework for both sides, correctness formalised as a *refinement relation*, and a syntactic simulation-based proof method run fully automatically. The certificate is the refinement proof; the validator ran after each compiler run.

**Necula, "Translation Validation for an Optimizing Compiler", PLDI 2000** (https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~necula/Papers/tv_pldi00.pdf). Validated GCC 2.7-2.91 pass by pass (branch optimisation, CSE, loop unrolling and inversion, register allocation, scheduling) on GCC itself and Linux 2.2. The certificate is a simulation relation between program points plus equality constraints on live registers and memory, *inferred* by symbolic evaluation and a hand-built solver rather than supplied by the compiler. Cost: validation of one pass took about four times the pass itself, doubled again by dumping and parsing RTL (section 6); 10,500 lines of OCaml, "about the effort of one compiler pass"; false alarms of up to 10% of functions for CSE and loop passes; it also flagged the failure modes that matter for us: memory reordering across calls and loop unrolling it "is not yet designed to handle".

**Rinard, "Credible Compilation", MIT-LCS-TR-776, 1999; Rinard and Marinov, "Credible Compilation with Pointers", FLoC RTRV 1999** (abridged version https://people.csail.mit.edu/rinard/paper/sma03.pdf; project page https://people.csail.mit.edu/rinard/paper/credibleCompilation.html). The compiler itself emits a proof in two parts: that its analysis result is correct (an invariant proof) and that a simulation relation holds between original and transformed program. A simple checker verifies it. This is the first relational program logic, and it is the design point closest to what we need, because our compiler knows the mapping it is constructing and does not need to have it rediscovered.

## 2. Where per-program validation scaled

**Alive** (Lopes, Menendez, Nagarakatte, Regehr, PLDI 2015, https://web.ist.utl.pt/nuno.lopes/pubs/alive-pldi15.pdf) covered 334 of LLVM's 1,028 InstCombine transformations and found 8 wrong ones; it verifies peepholes once, offline, not per program, and explicitly excludes floating point, control flow, loops and the semantics of the built-in predicates it trusts (section 2). **Alive2** (Lopes, Lee, Hur, Liu, Regehr, PLDI 2021, https://web.ist.utl.pt/nuno.lopes/pubs/alive2-pldi21.pdf; repository https://github.com/AliveToolkit/alive2) is genuine per-run *bounded* translation validation over LLVM IR: loops unrolled to a bound, no integer-to-pointer casts, one address space, locals assumed untouched by calls, non-terminating loops unsupported. Over the LLVM unit tests it reported 47 miscompilation bugs (28 fixed) at 2.5 hours per full run on 8 cores. Its lesson is that a validator works when both sides share one semantics and the gap per step is small; even then it checks *refinement* rather than equivalence because undefined behaviour makes equality the wrong relation. `llvm-diff` is only a syntactic alpha-equivalence check; Crellvm uses it as its equality oracle (below). **SeaHorn** (https://seahorn.github.io/) is a CHC-based verifier for LLVM programs, not a validator, and does not bear on the decision.

Two results bound what inference across a large gap can do. **Tristan, Govereau, Morrisett, PLDI 2011** (https://jtristan.github.io/papers/pldi11.pdf) validated LLVM's whole -O pipeline by normalising value graphs: 80% of functions overall, about 50% for GVN with no optimisation-specific rules, 19 minutes for gcc. **Gupta, Rose, Bansal, OOPSLA 2020** (https://sorav.compiler.ai/pubs/counter.pdf) infer product-CFG correlations and invariants between unoptimised IR and -O3 vectorised x86 by counterexample-guided search, with a five-hour budget per function and failures concentrated where transformations are not bisimilar. **Kasampalis et al., ASPLOS 2021 / thesis 2021** built KEQ, an equivalence checker parametric in two K semantics, on cut-bisimulation, and validated LLVM instruction selection and register allocation to x86 Machine IR at 91.5% and 96.7% of 4,732 SPEC functions (thesis abstract, https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/118300; the ASPLOS PDF at https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3445814.3446751 was not retrievable). KEQ is the tool most directly reusable here since both our semantics are already in K, and the thesis stresses that even KEQ needs compiler hints for instruction selection.

## 3. The contrast: verified compilers

**CompCert** (Leroy, "Formal verification of a realistic compiler", CACM 2009, https://xavierleroy.org/publi/compcert-CACM.pdf; https://compcert.org/compcert-C.html). The theorem is not equivalence. It is: for all S, C and behaviour B not in Wrong, if Comp(S)=OK(C) and S⇓B then C⇓B, where behaviours are termination, divergence or going wrong plus the I/O trace, proved as a forward simulation per pass and turned into behaviour inclusion by determinism of the target (section 2). The whole development at that time was 42,000 lines of Coq and about 3 person-years for Clight to PowerPC; parser, assembler and linker were outside the proof. **CakeML** (Kumar, Myreen, Norrish, Owens, POPL 2014, https://cakeml.org/popl14.pdf; Tan et al., ICFP 2016, https://cakeml.org/icfp16.pdf) proves observable-behaviour preservation through 12 intermediate languages down to machine code and bootstraps in the logic; the ICFP 2016 backend alone is about 100,000 lines of HOL4. What verification buys that validation cannot: completeness (the compiler never spuriously fails) and a single theorem that composes with source-level proofs for every program. Both papers also note that the proof must be redone when a pass changes.

## 4. The hybrid: verified validators

CompCert itself moved to this position. **Tristan and Leroy 2008** (https://xavierleroy.org/publi/validation-scheduling.pdf): verified validators for list and trace scheduling, 11,000 lines of Coq, one person-year, validation time comparable to the pass except AES (10x). **Tristan and Leroy 2009** (https://xavierleroy.org/publi/validation-LCM.pdf): lazy code motion, 5,000 lines of Coq, validator time 22.5% of the pass. **Rideau and Leroy 2010** (https://xavierleroy.org/publi/validation-regalloc.pdf): register allocation validator proved in 900 lines of Coq against more than 10,000 for the verified allocator, "a ten-fold reduction in proof effort" with 6% compile-time overhead; the cost is that the validator can reject a correct allocation. **Crellvm** (Kang et al., PLDI 2018, https://sf.snu.ac.kr/publications/crellvm.pdf) is the credible-compilation version for LLVM: mem2reg and GVN emit relational-Hoare proofs (213 and 440 lines of C++ of proof generation), checked by a checker verified in Coq against Vellvm (25,970 lines, of which 18,934 verify the checker); it found four bugs hidden for 7-8 years. **Sewell, Myreen, Klein, PLDI 2013** (https://trustworthy.systems/publications/nicta_full_text/6449.pdf) validated gcc -O1 binaries of seL4 against the C semantics via SMT (234 of 260 functions, 59 minutes; -O2 145 of 225, 4h23m), composing with the existing refinement stack. The pattern across all of these is the same: a small proven checker, an untrusted generator, and a relation that the generator provides.

## 5. The equivalence relation across the gap

Every success above relies on a stated relation, not on a discovered one. The theory is **Abadi and Lamport, "The Existence of Refinement Mappings", 1988** (https://lamport.azurewebsites.net/pubs/abadi-existence.pdf): when the low level implements the high level, a state-function refinement mapping exists after adding history and prophecy variables. Prophecy variables are exactly what a ZKIR witness is: values chosen "in advance" that a Core execution only determines later. **Ciobâcă, Lucanu, Rusu, Roșu, FAC 2016** (https://fsl.cs.illinois.edu/publications/ciobaca-lucanu-rusu-rosu-2016-faoc.pdf) give a language-independent proof system for full equivalence of programs in two different languages, parametric in both K semantics and a *state-similarity relation* supplied by the user, with a Circularity rule for loops; KEQ is its practical descendant. Tooling that constructs relations rather than checks them exists only for near-level pairs (Necula's heuristics, Gupta's counterexample search, KEQ's inference for register allocation) and degrades as the gap widens. The ZK-specific evidence confirms this: **Leo** (Chin, Wu, Coglio et al., 2021, https://eprint.iacr.org/2021/651.pdf) planned a verifying compiler generating an ACL2 theorem per compilation but reports only the forward direction (constraints imply specification) for a Blake2s gadget of 22,048 constraints in 3,100 seconds, with the backward direction (specification implies a witness exists) left as future work; **Coglio et al. 2023** (https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/1278.pdf) define gadget correctness as soundness plus completeness and verify snarkVM gadgets compositionally, per gadget, by hand-structured theorems. **CLAP** (Nethermind, 2026, https://www.nethermind.io/blog/clap-correctly-compiling-the-leanest-possible-circuits) and **vr1cs_lean** (https://github.com/lambdaclass/vr1cs_lean) both moved to Lean-verified lowering with per-transformation proofs, i.e. away from black-box inference, for the same reason. For us this means the relation must map Core financial state (balances, obligations, allowances) to named witness slots and constraint groups, and it must be two-sided: every Core step has a satisfying witness, and every satisfying witness corresponds to a Core step. The compiler is the only component that knows this mapping cheaply.

## Comparison

| Approach | Proof effort (once) | Per-program cost | When the compiler changes | Across a large abstraction gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Verified compiler (CompCert, CakeML) | 42k lines Coq / 3 person-years; ~100k lines HOL4 | none | proofs of the changed pass redone | works, but the simulation relation for every pass is written and proved by hand |
| Black-box validation (Necula, Alive2, Tristan 2011, Gupta 2020, KEQ) | 10k lines OCaml; Alive2 unverified C++ | 4x compile time (Necula); hours per suite (Alive2); up to 5 h per function (Gupta) | nothing, if the heuristics still fit; otherwise false alarms rise (Necula 10%, Tristan 20%) | 80-97% coverage at IR-to-machine; no published success at program-to-constraint-system |
| Verified validator, relation inferred (Tristan/Leroy, Rideau/Leroy) | 0.9k-11k lines Coq, about one person-year | 6%-25% of pass time, up to 10x on outliers | unchanged unless the transformation kind changes; spurious rejections possible | inference works only for structure-preserving passes |
| Credible compiler, relation emitted (Rinard, Crellvm, Leo plan) | checker proof 19k lines Coq (Crellvm); generator a few hundred lines per pass | checker linear in proof size; Leo 3,100 s per 22k-constraint gadget | generator updated with the pass; checker and theorem unchanged | the only approach whose relation is explicit and two-sided; recommended |
