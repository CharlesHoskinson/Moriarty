# What Turing incompleteness buys a financial contract DSL

The single advantage we can act on this quarter is this: because the core has no loops, recursion, calls or fixpoints and every collection is capped, the number of steps and the number of external inputs any program can ever consume is a function of its syntax tree alone. That makes the bounded-model-checking completeness threshold computable per program rather than guessed, so a symbolic execution of the program's own tree to that depth is a *proof* of a whole-behaviour property, not a bounded search. Marlowe has cashed exactly this out: `maxTransactionsInitialState c` is a syntactic function, Isabelle proves no accepted trace exceeds it (`playTrace_only_accepts_maxTransactionsInitialState`, `TransactionBound.thy`), and the SBV/Z3 static analyser uses that bound to decide "no payment ever fails for insufficient funds, no matter what inputs arrive" for a concrete contract. The recipe fits our ~15 constructors: write a symbolic interpreter over the concrete program (concrete map keys, symbolic values), normalise traces to one input per transaction (Marlowe proved this loses no counterexamples: `traceToSingleInputIsEquivalent`), and ask an SMT solver for a violating trace of length at most the syntactic bound. Add our work meter as a second symbolic property ("the meter never trips") and the meter's fail-closed branch becomes a statically discharged obligation instead of a runtime surprise. The practical ceiling is documented: kilobyte contracts in under a second, a fully expanded 19 MB four-party crowdfunding contract in about ten minutes (Lamela Seijas, Smith, Thompson 2020).

## 1. Financial DSLs that made the bet

**Peyton Jones, Eber, Seward (ICFP 2000).** Contracts are finite trees over a small combinator set (`one`, `give`, `and`, `or`, `truncate`, `then`, `scale`, `get`, `anytime`), with a compositional valuation semantics defined by structural recursion. What the finiteness bought was that every contract has a computable horizon and a valuation that is a fold over the tree; the paper's own implementation warning is that value-process lattices are "quadratic in the number of time steps" and "it would be Very Bad to fully" materialise them, so cost is bounded but not small. No verification tool is claimed; the payoff was pricing and lifecycle analysis by multiple interpretations.

**Frankau, Spinellis, Nassuphis, Burgard (JFP 2009), Barclays' FPF.** Direct descendant. "In fpf let bindings cannot be recursive - the language does not support general recursion. Instead we provide higher-order functions such as map and fold ... The fact that the language is not Turing-complete is not an impediment in practice and in many cases simplifies analysis." This is the strongest industrial endorsement of the design point. The paper's lessons section is also a warning: naive evaluation of shared sub-expressions was exponential until they added stable-name memoisation, so a non-recursive language still needs a cost discipline.

**Marlowe (Lamela Seijas, Nemish, Smith, Thompson; WTSC 2020; static analysis ISoLA 2020).** Excluded: recursion, unbounded waiting (every `When` carries a timeout), unbounded participants. Bought, and machine-checked in Isabelle (local vendored repo at `/home/charl/Moriarty/repos/marlowe/isabelle`, commit 7b5b1e9, 2026-05-26): `timedOutTransaction_closes_contract` and `timeOutTransaction_does_not_fail` (after `maxTimeContract c` an empty transaction always closes and refunds), the transaction bound above, `closeIsSafe`, `computeTransactionIsQuiescent`, and `computeTransaction_preserves_assets`. Decided by tooling that a general language cannot decide: the Playground analyser checks partial payments, non-positive deposits/payments and shadowed `Let`s "in advance of running a contract", plus user `Assert`s, over all input traces. The authors are explicit that this only works because "termination of a function is ... bounded by a concrete" syntactic quantity and because "the number of ways of partitioning inputs is exponential in the number of inputs" until traces are normalised.

**Findel (Biryukov, Khovratovich, Tikhomirov; WTSC 2017).** PJES combinators on Ethereum. Excluded loops and more than two parties; bought a declarative marketplace whose per-operation gas was measurable (10^5 to 10^6 gas). Verification was deferred ("formal semantics of Findel can be introduced ... this would enable formal verification") and delivered only later by Arusoaie (2020) in Coq, who also records the cost: "there is no way to encode repetitive behavior in contracts - there are no loops. This is a limitation when one wants to specify a contract should be executed repeatedly for 10 years."

**Daml (Bernauer et al. 2023).** The counter-example. Daml-LF top-level definitions "can be mutually recursive and therefore endow Daml-LF with unrestricted recursion and make it Turing complete"; non-monotonic datatypes even give a fixpoint combinator. Their stated reason: "Making Daml-LF total would not bear any practical advantages ... total languages do not exclude writing functions that take thousands of years to compute. Instead, ledger implementations may assign a cost to each Daml-LF evaluation step." Daml's 2016 non-Turing-complete claim was withdrawn (Canton forum, Verhaegen).

**Ergo (Roche, Hernandez, Chen, Siméon, Selman 2021).** Clauses are "an expression language with limited expressiveness (it allows conditional and bounded iteration)"; the compiler "guarantees error-free execution for well-typed Ergo programs" and is written in Coq "as a stepping stone for formal specification and verification". No contract-level theorem or analyser is reported; the payoff so far is compiler-level type safety.

**Simplicity (O'Connor 2017).** Not financial-specific but the cleanest statement of our thesis: being Turing incomplete, it "is amenable to static analysis that can be used to derive upper bounds on the computational resources needed, prior to execution", with Coq denotational semantics. This is precisely what our work meter should become: a statically computed number, not a runtime guard.

| Language | Excluded | What it bought | Cashed out in a tool? |
|---|---|---|---|
| PJES 2000 | recursion, open-ended combinators | compositional valuation, computable horizon | pricing engine only |
| FPF 2009 | general recursion | multi-interpretation analysis, lifecycle support | production at Barclays; no prover |
| Marlowe | recursion, timeout-less waiting | closability, transaction bound, asset preservation | yes: Isabelle proofs + SMT analyser |
| Findel | loops, >2 parties | declarative marketplace, gas measurable | later Coq semantics (Arusoaie 2020) |
| Daml | nothing (Turing complete) | expressiveness; cost model instead | interpreter metering |
| Ergo | recursion (bounded iteration only) | error-free execution of well-typed clauses | Coq compiler, no contract theorems |
| Simplicity | recursion | static resource upper bounds | Coq semantics, static analysis |

## 2. Total languages: the precise claim

Turner (2004) states the payoff exactly: in a total language reduction is *strongly* Church-Rosser, so "normal forms always exist and we can evaluate in any order", and equational reasoning on programs is sound without partiality side-conditions. Restricting recursion to syntactic descent yields Gödel's System T (primitive recursive functionals of finite type), which contains "every recursive function whose totality can be proved in first order logic"; the loss is that "an interpreter for L in L cannot be written", and whether all needed *algorithms* (not functions) survive is "an open question ... as it relates to complexity". Agda's checker admits only structural or lexicographic descent and is "intentionally conservative", with a `TERMINATING` escape hatch; Idris warns "the totality checker is not perfect". Dhall states the two decision procedures totality gives: "You can always type-check an expression in a finite amount of time" and "if an expression type-checks then evaluating that expression always succeeds in a finite amount of time", then immediately concedes "there are some short pathological programs that take longer than the heat death of the universe to evaluate".

Our design is stronger than all of these: with no recursion at all, we do not need a termination checker. Every program is a finite term; its evaluation cost is bounded by term size times the 128 collection cap, which is why the work meter can be a compile-time number.

## 3. Bounded model checking: where the bound sits

Biere, Cimatti, Clarke, Zhu (TACAS 1999) prove that for reachability, checking up to k = the diameter of the transition system is complete (Theorem 20), and the recurrence diameter suffices for all ECTL properties. Clarke, Kroening, Ouaknine, Strichman (VMCAI 2004) generalise this to a completeness threshold CT for any LTL property, but show computing CT in general is doubly exponential in the state variables, and note that "the leading cause for exponentially long loop-free paths is counters"; only about 25% of the hardware components they examined had diameter under 20. The honest number: a bounded state space is decidable but the completeness threshold is normally unknown and astronomically large. Our advantage is that a loop-free program has a syntactic diameter (Marlowe's `maxTransactions`), so k is known and small, and the SAT/SMT formula grows linearly in k. Marlowe's authors still call the resulting query "NP-complete in the general case" and needed normalisation and concrete map keys to get from minutes to sub-second.

## 4. Exhaustive testing as proof

Sullivan, Yang, Coppit, Khurshid, Jackson (ISSTA 2004) define bounded exhaustive testing as testing "for all valid inputs up to specified size bounds", grounded in Jackson's small-scope hypothesis, and report exhaustive coverage of about 250,000 fault-tree inputs at scope three. Andoni, Daniliuc, Khurshid, Marinov (2002) measured the hypothesis on Java collections: exhaustive testing within scopes of a few objects reached complete statement and branch coverage and killed almost all mutants. SmallCheck (Runciman, Naylor, Lindblad, Haskell 2008) implements depth-bounded exhaustive enumeration in Haskell; its own documentation is careful to call this testing, not proof. The precise claim we can make is narrower and stronger: over a *total* function on a *finite* domain, exhaustive enumeration is a proof by cases. The bound has to sit at the syntactic level (all programs to depth d, all traces to `maxTransactions`), not at the value level, because 128-element collections of integers already give a domain no enumerator will finish. Use enumeration to certify the interpreter and its cost model over small programs; use SMT (section 3) for values.

## 5. The counterweight

Three findings deserve weight. First, Marlowe's Isabelle money-preservation theorem was wrong for years: Tweag's 2023 audit found it "had only taken into account the amount of assets and not the type", so a contract receiving 20 ada and 15 djed and paying 20 djed and 15 ada satisfied the proof; the restriction bought decidability, not correct specifications. Second, the workaround: Marlowe users get repetition by unrolling, which is how a four-party crowdfunding contract becomes 19 MB and a ten-minute analysis; Findel simply cannot express a ten-year repeated payment. The hazard moves from the language into contract size and analysis time. Third, Daml abandoned the restriction outright, arguing totality gives no practical bound on time, and the Dhall community's own discussion concedes the same ("the absence of Turing completeness per se does not provide many safety guarantees"). The lesson for us: the payoff is real only if the bound is *small* and *syntactically computable*, which means resisting any future "repeat N" construct that reintroduces unrolling by another name.
