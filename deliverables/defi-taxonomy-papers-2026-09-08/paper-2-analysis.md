## Source taxonomy and semantic axes

The paper distinguishes protocol types from user strategies explicitly (§VII, p11). Lending protocols implement borrowing/lending primitives; yield farming is the user strategy of deploying capital for interest, fees and rewards; yield aggregators automate that strategy through vaults across underlying venues. An aggregator therefore is not another loan subtype. It composes lending markets and DEX pools and inherits dependencies such as bridges and oracles (§VII, pp11–13).

Table II (p3, visually checked) places **overcollateralized**, **undercollateralized**, and **zero-liquidation** loans under **collateral-based loans**, with **flashloans** as a separate column. These are useful comparison categories, but not a disjoint type hierarchy: zero-liquidation loans can also be overcollateralized; TrueFi is included under undercollateralized lending while its prose describes no collateral (§IV, pp7–8). Model collateral ratio and early-liquidation policy as separate facets when adapting the taxonomy.

| Table II facet | Overcollateralized | Undercollateralized | Zero liquidation | Flashloan |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Typical use | Speculation, hedging | Financing | Speculation, hedging | Arbitrage, liquidations |
| Duration | Indefinite | Fixed | Fixed | Immediate |
| Row named “Loan funding” | More than loan value | Less than loan value | More than loan value | None |
| Borrower rate | Dynamic | Fixed at borrowing | Fixed at borrowing | Fixed before borrowing |
| Lender return | Dynamic | Dynamic | Lender-set | Commission |
| Leverage characterization | Less than capital | More than capital | Less than capital | All available funds |

The “Loan funding” row is source terminology, apparently describing borrower collateral or upfront backing, not lender funding: a flashloan requires pool liquidity despite the cell “None.” Do not adopt this label as a capital-source type. Likewise, the table gives typical characteristics, not universal invariants; §III-A p5 explicitly permits fixed as well as variable rates and §IV p7 describes callable open-term Maple loans.

**Collateralized position lifecycle** (§III-A, pp3–5; Fig1a p3, Fig2 p4). Borrower supplies collateral, borrows against it, accrues interest, and either repays for collateral release or becomes eligible for liquidation. Falling collateral price, rising borrowed-asset price, and accruing interest can all breach the collateral-to-debt threshold. Top-ups reduce liquidation risk. Liquidation can be partial; eligibility does not guarantee an actor executes it. Price movement during execution delay and gas/liquidity costs matter. The simple diagram's terminated-loan alternatives are refined by this partial-liquidation prose.

**Zero-liquidation loans** (§III-C, pp5–6). Fixed maturity, fixed repayment amount, collateral, no early liquidation; maturity ends with repayment plus interest or forfeiture of collateral. High initial collateral ratios and rates compensate lenders for risk but do not eliminate the loss if collateral falls below debt. The borrower gains predictability and avoids continuous collateral monitoring. MYSO is the example; collateral eligibility depends on volatility and correlation. “Zero liquidation” means no liquidation before term, not no default/loss risk.

**Flashloans and flashswaps** (§III-B p5; §IV p7). No collateral, liquidity-limited principal, atomic repayment-or-revert and principal-based fee rather than duration-based interest. Uses include arbitrage, liquidations, collateral switching and attack financing. Flashswaps allow repayment with the other pool asset, subject to AMM constraints and swap fee. The p5 constant-product example is `x*y=k`, with x and y token quantities; it is a stylized reserve invariant, not a complete fee-aware adapter implementation.

**Permissioned credit and callable loans** (§IV pp7–8). Maple is described as moving from over/undercollateralized institutional lending toward overcollateralized lending in 2023; KYC and financial disclosures support risk assessment. Its Syrup service is described as permissionless. Maple's open-term loans can be called with 14–30 days' notice; fixed loan rate need not imply a fixed noncallable maturity. TrueFi uses governance approval/risk assessment and staked TRU loss absorption; borrower rates have a borrower-set cap, lender returns depend on utilization. These are paper-specific historical descriptions, not verification of current deployments.

**Liquidation mechanism axis** (§V-C p8). First-come-first-served execution rewards speed and involves bots, transaction ordering and potentially flashloan funding. Auctions trade slower execution for better protocol recovery. Type I bad debt means collateral no longer covers debt; type II means liquidation reward cannot justify transaction fees, potentially changing with gas prices. Eligibility, execution incentive and realized recovery are different predicates. Discount to liquidator versus retained protocol penalty is a policy choice; the paper's split should not be generalized to all protocols.

**Aggregator mechanism** (§VII pp11–13). A user chooses an aggregator and one or more vaults, deposits tokens, and holds a claim while the vault deploys capital, rebalances, harvests and reinvests. Yield farming commonly issues receipt tokens (AMM LP tokens or lending interest-bearing tokens) that represent pool shares and support redemption. Aggregators pool participants so the transaction cost of compounding can be shared. Aggregator, vault, strategy, underlying venue, receipt token and reward asset are distinguishable entities. Vault strategy and chain coverage vary; TVL concentration and dependency footprint are risk-relevant comparisons, not established explanations of success.

## Interest, units, state updates, and clocks

In §III-A (pp4–5), utilization `U` is borrowed funds as a fraction of available pool funds, assumed in `[0,1]`; borrow rate `i_b` and supply rate `i_s` differ. The paper gives `i_s(U)=i_b(U)*U` and reserve-adjusted `i_s(U)=i_b(U)*U*(1-lambda)`, where lambda is a reserve factor. These formulas assume a shared time basis, contemporaneous state and comparable units; the paper does not formalize rounding, edge cases with no funds, reward-token subsidy, reserve accounting, or exact denominator construction. Multiplication by U reflects that supplied funds need not all be borrowed; it should not by itself be read as protocol profit. Section IX-C (pp14–15) treats reserves as loss buffers and discusses asset caps, tighter collateral requirements and risk assessment.

Three model families (§III-A p5):

1. Linear: `i_b(U)=alpha+beta*U`.
2. Kinked: `alpha+beta*U` below `U*`; `alpha+beta*U*+gamma*(U-U*)` at or above `U*`. The expression is continuous at the kink and gamma is the upper-segment slope as written, despite prose calling it an “increase” in slope. A steeper upper segment requires gamma > beta. Do not implement gamma as beta+gamma without a separate convention.
3. Nonlinear: `alpha*U+beta*U^b+gamma*U^c`; exponents can be 32 and 64. Equation (2), §IV p7, instantiates dYdX with `(70*U+10*U^32+20*U^64)*2^-10`.

Equation (1), §IV p6, gives Compound's ETH pool kink 0.9 with base `3.17e-10`, lower slope `4.91e-10`, upper slope `3.99e-8`. The nearby annual-percent plot (Fig3 p7) and versions mentioned in the prose require care: the paper does not fully specify every conversion, deployment binding and rate period in these expressions. Treat these as paper-reported model examples rather than executable current parameters.

Clock and accrual differ (§IV pp6–7; §VIII-A/B p13): AAVE is described using per-block timestamps and lazy accrual when balance reads/updates happen; Compound is described counting blocks under a fixed inter-block-time assumption. Block timestamps and block counts each bring chain-specific assumptions. Comparisons across venues can be skewed when their clocks disagree. The paper cites mining-rate manipulation (“stretching and squeezing”) and utilization manipulation by liquidity supply adjustments as distinct attacks. Governance-controlled MakerDAO stability fees (§IV p7) are another rate-setting mechanism, distinct from an automatic utilization function.

Flashloan fees are amount-based (§III-B p5). Listed AAVE and Uniswap percentages are historical source claims and were not verified against current versions; they must not be silently substituted into language defaults. A fee paid in an atomic operation is not an annual rate with tiny elapsed time.

**Explicit resolution of the block/transaction wording issue.** The earlier capsule calls it a block/transaction conflict. In the supplied byte-identical PDF the abstract (p1) does not state a repayment boundary; the introduction specifies a single atomic operation. The conflict is internal to §III-B (p5): its overview defines a single transaction, yet says repayment is checked “within the block,” and its illustration allows a short transaction sequence and invokes shared timestamps. The interest paragraph returns to single-transaction atomicity. Section IV (p7) explicitly describes MakerDAO and Uniswap settlement within the same transaction. The strongest consistent reading is repayment-or-revert scoped to the encompassing transaction. A shared block timestamp does not establish joint rollback across otherwise separate transactions. Preserve the conflicting text as a source defect; do not claim this paper establishes block-wide atomic loans. A language proposal should require a transaction-scoped repayment obligation, with any cross-transaction composition needing its own independently specified atomicity mechanism. This is an interpretive resolution of the paper, not a live implementation audit.

## Evidence and its limits

Section VI (pp8–11, Tables IV–VI, Figs4–5) reports The Graph subgraph events and snapshots for Ethereum Compound V2 (May 7 2019–Jan 31 2024; blocks 7710833–19129417) and AAVE V2 (Nov 30 2020–Jan 31 2024; blocks 11363052–19129823). Collection uses monthly timestamp filters, deterministic ascending entity-id pagination with 1000-row pages, and flattened CSV outputs; analysis code is referenced as [53], not executed here. Event kinds: borrow, repay, deposit, withdraw, liquidation. A repayment can come from another address; liquidation applies to borrower aggregate debt, not a particular borrow event. The paper explicitly uses addresses rather than unique users.

Reported Compound/AAVE borrower-address counts are 29,768/53,514; at-least-once-liquidated shares 15.64%/11.29%; reported average collateral/debt ratios 348.5%/333.3%. These empirical averages are not thresholds or universal borrower preferences. Table VI's paired event gaps have median deposit→borrow 21.84/7.06 minutes and borrow→repay 7.01/22.89 days. They describe recorded event pairs, not a proved loan-duration distribution or identity-resolved behavior. The paper's hypotheses about the unusually small AAVE repayment stream and V3 migration remain hypotheses. Deposits/withdrawals and pool supply/collateral roles require deployment-specific decoding; the prose's blanket withdrawal restriction is not a complete account health rule.

Tables III/VII/VIII (pp6,12) contain January 2025 protocol/vault snapshots and August 2022 aggregator comparisons, separate from the event study ending January 2024. Selection is representative rather than exhaustive. Section VII-B p13 says different aggregator sources supplied TVL and APY without normalization. Table VIII labels APY “% per 30D” whereas prose defines annual percentage yield: rate horizon or reporting-window meaning is unresolved and must not be normalized by assumption. The paper expressly says snapshot market-share comparisons do not establish causality (§VII-A pp12–13). Automation, chain coverage, vault concentration and dependency footprint are proposed explanatory dimensions for future work.

Further internal inconsistencies worth retaining as quality caveats: Table III says dYdX lending ended Nov 2021 whereas §IV p7 says disabled in 2022; Table VII places Badger fourth in 2022 while §VII-A p12 says third. Table I's naming of reference [10] and §VII's “Xu and Reap” do not match the reference-list Xu/Feng authorship (p15). These do not erase the taxonomy but limit precise metadata/snapshot reuse.

## Security and open questions

Source findings (§VIII pp13–14; §IX pp14–15): (a) inaccurate time measurement; (b) intentional mining-rate changes and utilization supply shocks; (c) governance concentration, poor informed participation and airdrop farming; (d) slow governance updates versus urgent collateral-risk changes, illustrated by a week-long Compound collateral-factor adjustment; (e) reward-driven simultaneous borrowing/depositing and borrowed governance-token voting power; (f) code exploits, oracle/asset price shocks, illiquid collateral and slippage that defeat liquidation profitability. Section IX-A extrapolates timing attacks to PoS skipped slots and proposes combining with RANDAO manipulation; this is an open research direction, not a reproduced exploit or measured profitability result. A nominal 12-second slot schedule is not identical to consecutive produced-block timing.

Revenue-based financing (§IX-D p15) is presented as a possible unsecured loan repaid from a monthly revenue fraction. Income observability and account diversion defeat collection. The paper's “not implemented” wording is a bounded author knowledge claim at writing time, not a verified current negative. Its financing family lacks a complete enforceable on-chain specification.

## Proposed language implications — researcher proposals, not source claims

1. Represent positions with orthogonal fields: collateralization ratio/backing policy; maturity/callability; early-liquidation eligibility; repayment/forfeiture alternatives; borrower and lender rate policy; permission/credit-assessment authority. This avoids forcing a zero-liquidation overcollateralized term loan into one exclusive class (Table II, §III-C, §IV).
2. Separate `Rate<TimeBasis>` from `Fee<Principal>` and declare compounding convention, reserve split, rate-update authority, utilization definition and version-bound curve parameters. Require explicit conversions across timestamp, block-height and slot clocks (§III-A/B; §IV; §VIII-A/B).
3. Treat atomic flash settlement as a transaction-scoped obligation with fee, repayment asset and final-balance predicate. Distinguish a same-asset flashloan from a reserve-invariant flashswap. Do not infer cross-transaction atomicity from same-block timestamps (§III-B contradiction; §IV).
4. Separate liquidation eligibility from an external execution action, auction state, partial repayment, proceeds allocation and bad-debt outcome. A safety property about collateral coverage needs price, execution-delay, liquidity, gas and actor assumptions; syntax cannot guarantee liquidation liveness (§III-A; §V-C; §IX-B).
5. Type vault receipt claims and underlying assets separately; express harvest, swap, reinvest, rebalance and redemption as composable operations with dependency contracts. Record bridge/oracle/venue dependencies and chain scope so strategy composition carries risk assumptions (§VII).
6. Separate protocol rewards from interest and from voting rights. Rewarded recursive borrowing can affect both economic return and governance power; time delays and voting snapshots need explicit temporal semantics (§VIII-C/D/E).
7. Use historical measurements only to prioritize examples, workloads and stress scenarios. Every measurement should preserve protocol version, chain, source window, event/position/address entity type and stated units. APY ambiguity stays unresolved until source-specific clarification (§VI; §VII).

These are candidate abstractions and proof obligations inferred from the inspected source. This source analysis is connected to the existing Moriarty design in [the design comparison](DESIGN-IMPLICATIONS.md); its proposals remain S2.
